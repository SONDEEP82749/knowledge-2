Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first academic landscape, libraries are increasingly relying on algorithmic systems to improve user experience, recommend resources, and streamline access to information. However, these systems—while powerful—are not immune to algorithmic bias, a phenomenon where automated decision-making processes disproportionately favor or disadvantage certain groups based on factors like race, gender, socioeconomic status, or geographic location.

A recent study by the Pew Research Center (2023) found that 68% of academic librarians report concerns about bias in digital library systems, with 42% citing search result disparities as a major issue. Meanwhile, a 2022 report from the American Library Association (ALA) highlighted that 37% of library users feel their search queries are being filtered in ways that limit access to diverse perspectives.

One institution at the forefront of addressing this challenge is the Auraria Library, a collaborative academic library serving the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver. Recognizing the ethical and practical implications of algorithmic bias, Auraria Library has taken proactive steps to audit, refine, and democratize its digital systems. This blog post explores their approach, offering actionable strategies for other libraries looking to mitigate bias in their own systems.

Understanding Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before diving into solutions, it’s essential to grasp what algorithmic bias means in a library context. Unlike traditional discrimination, which is often intentional, algorithmic bias arises from unintended flaws in data, training models, or system design. Here are some key ways bias manifests in library algorithms:

1. Search Result Bias

  • Example: A search for "women in STEM" might return fewer results about women in STEM fields compared to generic "STEM" searches, reinforcing stereotypes.
  • Why it happens: Training data may lack representation of women in technical fields, or ranking algorithms prioritize historically dominant topics.

2. Recommendation System Bias

  • Example: A user frequently recommended books by white male authors, even when they expressed interest in diverse voices.
  • Why it happens: Collaborative filtering algorithms (which suggest items based on similar users’ preferences) can reinforce echo chambers if the initial user base is homogeneous.

3. Access Restriction Bias

  • Example: Certain databases or e-journals are more accessible to users from wealthier institutions, limiting equitable access.
  • Why it happens: Licensing agreements or subscription models may favor larger universities, creating a digital divide.

4. Language and Terminology Bias

  • Example: A search for "African American history" might return fewer results than "Black history," even though both terms are valid.
  • Why it happens: Algorithms trained on historical data may associate certain terms with lower relevance due to semantic gaps.

5. Geographic and Institutional Bias

  • Example: A library’s discovery system may prioritize resources from Ivy League institutions, marginalizing contributions from community colleges or global scholars.
  • Why it happens: Citation metrics and journal rankings often favor elite institutions, reinforcing academic hierarchies.

Auraria Library’s response to these challenges involves a multi-layered approach, combining technical audits, ethical guidelines, and user-centered design. Below, we’ll explore their strategies in detail.

Auraria Library’s Framework for Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library’s work is guided by three core principles:

  1. Transparency – Making algorithmic decisions explainable.
  2. Inclusivity – Ensuring diverse perspectives are represented.
  3. Continuous Improvement – Regularly testing and refining systems.

Their approach can be broken down into eight key strategies that other libraries can adapt.

Strategy 1: Conducting Algorithmic Impact Assessments (AIAs)

What it is: A structured review of how algorithms affect different user groups, similar to an environmental impact study but for digital systems.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Step 1: Define User Segments – They categorize users by demographics, institutional affiliation, and search behavior (e.g., undergraduate vs. graduate, first-generation students vs. legacy students).
  • Step 2: Test for Bias – Using A/B testing, they compare search results for queries from different groups. For example, they might test whether a search for "mental health" returns different results for a user identifying as LGBTQ+ versus a cisgender user.
  • Step 3: Document Findings – They publish anonymous reports (without violating privacy) to identify patterns, such as:
    • "Users from community colleges receive fewer recommendations for high-impact journals compared to university students."
    • "Searches for Indigenous perspectives yield lower relevance scores than Eurocentric topics."

Why it works:

  • Data-driven decisions replace guesswork.
  • Proactive adjustments prevent bias from becoming entrenched.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries:

  • Start with one high-traffic algorithm (e.g., your discovery layer or recommendation engine).
  • Use open-source bias detection tools like IBM’s AI Fairness 360 or Google’s What-If Tool to analyze disparities.
  • Document biases in a public-facing report (even if preliminary) to hold your team accountable.

Strategy 2: Diversifying Training Data for Search Algorithms

What it is: Ensuring the data used to train search and recommendation algorithms reflects real-world diversity rather than historical imbalances.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Curate Inclusive Datasets – They partner with diversity experts to expand training data beyond traditional academic sources. For example:
    • They augment their catalog with open-access repositories like JSTOR Forum (which includes global perspectives).
    • They manually seed searches for underrepresented topics (e.g., "Latinx women in physics") to improve relevance scores.
  • Use Bias Mitigation Techniques – They apply reweighting algorithms to adjust for underrepresented groups in training data.
  • Leverage Crowdsourcing – They run pilot programs where librarians and students tag and annotate marginalized voices in historical collections.

Real-World Example: Before implementing these changes, a search for "Black feminism" in Auraria’s discovery system often returned fewer results than "feminist theory." After diversifying training data and adjusting ranking algorithms, the system now prioritizes intersectional feminist scholarship, including works by authors like Angela Davis and Kimberlé Crenshaw.

Common Mistake & How to Avoid It: ❌ Assuming "neutral" data is unbiased – Many libraries rely on traditional academic databases, which historically favor Western, male, and elite perspectives. ✅ Solution: Actively curate diverse datasets and audit them regularly. Partner with disability studies, ethnic studies, and LGBTQ+ scholars to identify gaps.

Strategy 3: Implementing Fairness-Aware Ranking Algorithms

What it is: Using algorithms that explicitly account for fairness alongside relevance, rather than optimizing for clicks alone.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Multi-Objective Ranking – Instead of ranking results purely by click-through rate (CTR), they use a weighted system that balances:
    • Relevance (traditional ranking)
    • Diversity (ensuring a mix of perspectives)
    • Fairness (avoiding disproportionate exclusion of certain groups)
  • Example: If a search for "climate justice" returns mostly Western academic papers, the algorithm boosts results from Global South scholars to ensure representation.
  • Dynamic Adjustments – They use reinforcement learning to continuously refine rankings based on user feedback, ensuring fairness doesn’t become static.

Actionable Tip:

  • Experiment with fairness metrics in your discovery layer. Tools like Fairlearn (by Microsoft Research) can help implement constrained optimization for ranking.
  • Start small – Apply fairness adjustments to one subject area (e.g., social sciences) before scaling.

Strategy 4: Training Staff on Algorithmic Bias & Ethical AI

What it is: Ensuring librarians and IT staff understand how bias works and their role in mitigating it.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Workshops & Certifications – They offer monthly training sessions on:
    • How search algorithms work (beyond just "plug-and-play" tools).
    • Recognizing bias in data (e.g., gendered language in metadata).
    • Ethical AI principles (e.g., ALA’s Code of Ethics, Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency in AI).
  • Cross-Department Collaboration – Librarians work with data scientists, IT, and faculty to co-design bias mitigation strategies.
  • Case Studies – They discuss real-world examples of algorithmic harm (e.g., Amazon’s biased hiring tool, Google’s search ranking controversies) to spark critical thinking.

Real-World Example: Before training, Auraria’s reference desk received complaints that "search results for Indigenous topics were often outdated or irrelevant." After staff training, they audited metadata tags and recommended diversifying subject headings (e.g., adding "Indigenous environmental studies" as a separate category).

Common Mistake & How to Avoid It: ❌ Assuming tech teams alone can solve bias – Many libraries leave algorithmic governance to IT departments, ignoring the human element. ✅ Solution: Embed ethical AI training in onboarding and annual reviews for all staff. Encourage interdisciplinary teams to review algorithms.

Strategy 5: Developing User Feedback Loops for Continuous Improvement

What it is: Actively listening to users to identify where algorithms fail marginalized groups.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Anonymous Feedback Forms – They include a bias reporting option in their discovery system, allowing users to flag:
    • "I searched for 'Asian American literature' but got mostly white authors."
    • "The recommendations kept suggesting books by the same few authors."
  • Surveys with Demographic Questions – They ask users:
    • "Do you feel the search results reflect your interests?"
    • "Have you noticed any groups being underrepresented?"
  • Community Advisory Boards – They partner with student groups (e.g., Black Student Union, Disability Alliance) to test algorithms and provide feedback.

Actionable Tip:

  • Pilot a "Bias Alert" feature in your catalog or ILS (Integrated Library System).
  • Share aggregated feedback (without PII) with your team to prioritize fixes.

Strategy 6: Advocating for Open Access & Decolonial Data Sources

What it is: Reducing reliance on proprietary, biased databases by integrating open, inclusive alternatives.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Expanding Open Access Subscriptions – They negotiate licenses for open-access journals (e.g., PLOS, Directory of Open Access Books) to ensure global and marginalized voices are included.
  • Partnering with Indigenous & Decolonial Repositories – They add Indigenous knowledge databases (e.g., Indigenous Studies Portal) and decolonial archives to their discovery layer.
  • Promoting Alternative Metrics – They encourage faculty to use altmetrics (e.g., social media engagement, community impact) alongside traditional citation counts, which often favor elite institutions.

Real-World Example: Before this shift, Auraria’s psychology database heavily featured Western psychological theories, with little representation of Afrocentric or Indigenous psychology. After integrating open-access repositories like African Journals Online, searches for "Afrocentric therapy" now return relevant, peer-reviewed sources from African scholars.

Common Mistake & How to Avoid It: ❌ Assuming proprietary databases are "neutral" – Many libraries default to expensive, biased vendors without questioning their content. ✅ Solution: Audit your database subscriptions for geographic, racial, and disciplinary biases. Negotiate for open-access alternatives where possible.

Strategy 7: Implementing Bias-Aware Metadata Tagging

What it is: Ensuring library metadata (subject headings, keywords) actively includes diverse perspectives rather than relying on outdated taxonomies.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Updating Subject Headings – They replace exclusionary terms (e.g., "handicapped" → "people with disabilities") and add intersectional tags (e.g., "Black women in science").
  • Crowdsourced Tagging – They run community tagging events where students and faculty suggest new keywords for underrepresented topics.
  • Cross-Referencing with Global Thesauri – They use Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH) alongside controlled vocabularies from global libraries (e.g., National Library of Australia’s Indigenous terms).

Actionable Tip:

  • Run a "Metadata Audit" – Use tools like Zotero’s keyword analysis or Excel pivot tables to identify underrepresented subjects.
  • Train catalogers on inclusive metadata practices.

Strategy 8: Transparency Reports & Public Accountability

What it is: Publicly disclosing how algorithms work and their impact on different groups to build trust.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Annual Algorithmic Transparency Reports – They publish non-technical summaries of:
    • Which user groups are most affected by bias?
    • What steps are being taken to fix it?
    • How users can provide feedback.
  • Interactive Dashboards – They create public-facing tools (e.g., a bias impact calculator) where users can see:
    • "If you’re a first-gen student, here’s how our search algorithm differs from a legacy student’s experience."
  • Community Town Halls – They host quarterly discussions with students, faculty, and local advocates to co-create solutions.

Real-World Example: Auraria’s 2023 Transparency Report revealed that Latino/a/x students were 20% less likely to receive recommendations for STEM resources compared to white students. In response, they expanded their Latino/a/x STEM database subscriptions and created a "STEM for All" recommendation filter.

Common Mistake & How to Avoid It: ❌ Keeping algorithmic decisions secret – Many libraries treat AI as a "black box," making it hard to trust. ✅ Solution: Publish regular transparency reports (even if preliminary). Engage with the community to co-design fixes.

Strategy 9: Legal & Policy Frameworks for Algorithmic Fairness

What it is: Structuring policies to ensure legal compliance and ethical accountability in algorithmic decision-making.

How Auraria Library does it:

  • Algorithmic Bias Policy – They adopted a formal policy stating:
    • "All library algorithms must undergo fairness testing before deployment."
    • "Users have the right to request algorithmic impact assessments."
