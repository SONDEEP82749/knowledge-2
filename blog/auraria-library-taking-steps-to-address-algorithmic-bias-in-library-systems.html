Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first world, libraries—especially academic institutions like Auraria Library—rely heavily on algorithmic systems to improve user experience, recommend resources, and streamline access to information. However, these systems are not immune to algorithmic bias, a growing concern that can disproportionately affect marginalized communities, researchers, and students.

A 2023 report by the American Library Association (ALA) found that 68% of academic librarians acknowledge bias in their institution’s digital systems, yet only 32% have implemented formal strategies to mitigate it. Meanwhile, a 2022 study in Library Trends revealed that 45% of students reported feeling excluded from library resources due to algorithmic recommendations that favored certain disciplines or authors over others.

Auraria Library, part of the University of Colorado Denver (UCD) system, has taken proactive steps to address these issues. By recognizing the ethical and practical implications of biased algorithms, the library is working to ensure that all users—regardless of background, discipline, or identity—have equitable access to knowledge.

This blog post explores how Auraria Library is tackling algorithmic bias, the real-world impact of such biases, and actionable strategies that other libraries can adopt. Whether you're a librarian, researcher, or student, understanding these issues is crucial for fostering inclusive, fair, and effective library systems.

Understanding Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before diving into solutions, it’s essential to grasp what algorithmic bias is and how it manifests in library environments.

What Is Algorithmic Bias?

Algorithmic bias occurs when machine-learning models, recommendation engines, or search algorithms produce unfair or discriminatory outcomes based on historical data, training biases, or systemic inequalities. In libraries, this can lead to:

  • Over-representation of certain authors, genres, or disciplines (e.g., favoring Western scholars over global perspectives).
  • Exclusion of underrepresented voices (e.g., marginalizing LGBTQ+ literature or Indigenous knowledge).
  • Disparities in resource recommendations (e.g., students in STEM fields getting fewer humanities recommendations and vice versa).
  • Access barriers for non-native English speakers (e.g., search algorithms prioritizing English-language results).

How Does This Affect Auraria Library Users?

Auraria Library serves a diverse student body, including:

  • First-generation college students
  • International students
  • Students from low-income backgrounds
  • Researchers in interdisciplinary fields

If algorithms favor certain search terms, authors, or publication dates, they may systematically disadvantage users who rely on these systems for research, education, and personal growth.

For example:

  • A first-gen student searching for financial aid resources might receive fewer relevant results if the algorithm is trained on data from privileged students who already know where to look.
  • A researcher studying African diaspora literature might struggle to find non-Western sources if the recommendation engine defaults to European and American authors.
  • A non-native English speaker might face language barriers if the search algorithm prioritizes English-only metadata.

Auraria Library recognizes that fairness in algorithms is not just an ethical concern—it’s a necessity for academic equity.

Auraria Library’s Approach to Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library has implemented multiple strategies to detect, audit, and mitigate bias in its systems. Below, we break down key initiatives and actionable steps that other libraries can adopt.

1. Conducting Bias Audits on Search & Recommendation Algorithms

Problem: Many library search engines (e.g., WorldCat, Koha, Alma) use proprietary algorithms that may reinforce biases without transparency.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Partnered with data scientists to audit search algorithms for demographic disparities.
  • Tested query performance across different user groups (e.g., comparing search results for STEM vs. humanities students).
  • Discovered that 28% of search results for social sciences queries favored male-dominated authors, while gender-diverse authors were underrepresented.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ Use open-source tools like Fairlearn (Microsoft) or Aequitas (IBM) to audit your search algorithms. ✅ Benchmark search performance by user demographics (e.g., major, language, location). ✅ Request transparency reports from library system vendors (e.g., ProQuest, EBSCO) on algorithm training data.

2. Diversifying Training Data for AI Recommendations

Problem: If an AI recommendation system is trained on historically biased data, it will reinforce those biases.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Expanded metadata to include more diverse authors, publishers, and topics.
  • Added bias mitigation layers by weighting recommendations toward underrepresented disciplines (e.g., Indigenous studies, feminist theory).
  • Result: After adjustments, LGBTQ+ literature recommendations increased by 42% for queer students.

Real-World Example: At Harvard’s Houghton Library, researchers found that their recommendation engine favored 19th-century European texts over African and Asian literature. By rebalancing training data, they improved global literature visibility by 35%.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ Curate a "bias-check" dataset with equal representation of genders, ethnicities, and disciplines. ✅ Use controlled vocabulary (e.g., LCSH, MeSH) to explicitly include marginalized perspectives. ✅ Regularly update training data to reflect emerging research trends.

3. Implementing Fairness Constraints in Search Ranking

Problem: Traditional PageRank-like algorithms often favor popularity, which can exclude niche but valuable research.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Modified ranking algorithms to prioritize relevance over recency or citation count.
  • Introduced "fairness constraints" to penalize over-representation of certain authors while boosting underrepresented ones.
  • Example: If J.K. Rowling’s works dominate fantasy recommendations, the system now mixes in works by Black and Latinx authors.

Real-World Example: MIT Libraries found that their journal recommendation system favored STEM journals, leading humanities students to miss relevant articles. By applying fairness constraints, they balanced recommendations by discipline, improving user satisfaction by 30%.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ Use fairness-aware ranking models (e.g., FairRank, FairMatch). ✅ Set "diversity thresholds" (e.g., "No more than 60% of recommendations can come from the same author"). ✅ Allow users to adjust fairness settings (e.g., "Show me more diverse results").

4. Training Staff on Algorithmic Bias & Ethical AI

Problem: Even well-intentioned librarians may unconsciously reinforce bias if they don’t understand how algorithms work.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Hosted workshops on algorithmic fairness, bias in data, and ethical AI.
  • Created a "Bias Response Team" to audit new systems before deployment.
  • Result: 85% of staff now recognize bias in their daily workflows.

Real-World Example: University of California Berkeley’s Doe Library trained reference librarians to spot biased search results and guide users toward fairer alternatives. This led to a 20% increase in student satisfaction with digital resources.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ **Offer certification courses on algorithmic fairness (e.g., via Coursera, edX). ✅ Form a "Bias Task Force" with librarians, data scientists, and diversity advocates. ✅ **Conduct role-playing exercises where staff identify and correct biased recommendations.

5. Providing User Controls for Personalized Search

Problem: One-size-fits-all algorithms can exclude users who don’t fit the "average" profile.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Added "fairness toggles" in the search interface (e.g., "Show me diverse results").
  • Allowed users to adjust bias mitigation levels (e.g., "Reduce author bias," "Increase language diversity").
  • Result: International students reported a 38% improvement in finding multilingual resources.

Real-World Example: New York Public Library (NYPL) introduced "FairSearch"—a customizable search mode that adjusts results based on user preferences. Users could prioritize books by women, POC, or non-English authors, leading to a 40% increase in diverse book checkouts.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ Add bias adjustment sliders in your catalog system. ✅ Offer language preference filters for non-native speakers. ✅ Allow users to save their fairness settings for future searches.

7. Using Open-Source & Transparent Tools

Problem: Proprietary library systems (e.g., Alma, Koha) often lack transparency in their algorithms.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • **Migrated some systems to open-source alternatives (e.g., Koha, Evergreen) where algorithms can be audited.
  • **Used Fairlearn to monitor bias in real time.
  • Result: Full visibility into how recommendations are generated.

Real-World Example: University of Minnesota Libraries switched to open-source catalog software and audited their search algorithm, discovering that certain ethnic studies books were being deprioritized. By adjusting ranking weights, they improved accessibility by 32%.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ **Explore open-source library systems (e.g., Koha, Evergreen, Folio). ✅ **Use bias detection tools like Fairlearn, Aequitas. ✅ **Demand transparency from vendors on algorithm training data.

8. Educating Users About Algorithmic Bias

Problem: Users may not realize that algorithms can be biased and how to navigate them fairly.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • **Created guided tutorials on how to find diverse resources.
  • **Added bias awareness badges in search results (e.g., "This result may favor Western perspectives").
  • **Hosted workshops on critical information literacy.

Real-World Example: University of Toronto Libraries introduced "Bias-Busting Search Guides" that helped students recognize biased results. This led to a 22% increase in students seeking alternative sources.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ **Develop interactive guides on fair search strategies. ✅ **Use pop-up alerts to flag potentially biased results. ✅ **Offer workshops on evaluating algorithmic fairness.

9. Advocating for Policy Changes in Library Tech

Problem: Many library systems are built on outdated assumptions about what "good" research looks like.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • **Lobbied for inclusive metadata standards (e.g., more diverse subject headings).
  • **Worked with publishers to improve representation in databases.
  • Result: More Indigenous and global perspectives are now visible in search results.

Real-World Example: University of California System pushed for **more inclusive Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH), leading to **better representation of LGBTQ+ and racial justice topics.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ **Advocate for more diverse metadata in library databases. ✅ **Partner with publishers to expand global perspectives. ✅ **Push for policy changes in library tech standards.

10. Measuring & Iterating on Fairness Metrics

Problem: Bias is not static—it evolves as new data is added.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • **Track fairness metrics (e.g., author diversity, language representation, discipline balance).
  • **Set quarterly audits to check for new biases.
  • Adjust algorithms based on real-time feedback.

Real-World Example: University of Edinburgh Libraries continuously monitored their recommendation engine and **found that Scottish authors were underrepresented. By adjusting weights, they balanced representation by region.

Actionable Tip for Other Libraries: ✅ **Set up automated bias monitoring (e.g., Fairlearn dashboards). ✅ **Conduct quarterly fairness audits. ✅ **Use user feedback to refine algorithms.

Common Mistakes in Addressing Algorithmic Bias (And How to Avoid Them)

While Auraria Library’s approach is proactive and effective, many institutions make critical errors when trying to combat bias. Here’s what not to do:

❌ Mistake 1: Assuming Bias Doesn’t Exist in Your System

Why it’s bad: Many librarians believe their systems are neutral until a major incident occurs. Solution: Audit your algorithms regularly—bias is often subtle but persistent.

❌ Mistake 2: Relying Only on Vendor Solutions

Why it’s bad: Proprietary systems may hide bias behind "black-box" algorithms. Solution: Use open-source tools and demand transparency from vendors.

❌ Mistake 3: Ignoring User Feedback

Why it’s bad: Users often notice bias before librarians do. Solution: Encourage feedback and act on it—don’t dismiss complaints as "user error."

❌ Mistake 4: Overcorrecting for Bias (Creating New Biases)

Why it’s bad: If you artificially boost underrepresented groups too much, you may distort relevance. Solution: Use fairness-aware algorithms that balance diversity and accuracy.
