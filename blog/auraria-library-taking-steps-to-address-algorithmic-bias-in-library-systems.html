Why Algorithmic Bias in Libraries Is a Critical Issue

Before exploring solutions, it’s essential to understand why algorithmic bias is problematic in academic libraries.

1. Reinforcing Existing Inequities

Libraries are gateways to education and opportunity, but biased algorithms can amplify disparities rather than reduce them. For example:

  • If a search algorithm favors books by white male authors, students from underrepresented backgrounds may miss out on diverse perspectives in their research.
  • Recommendation systems that prioritize popular (often mainstream) titles over niche or scholarly works by marginalized voices limit intellectual exposure.

2. Undermining the Core Mission of Libraries

The American Library Association (ALA) defines libraries as "engines of equity," ensuring that all users—regardless of race, gender, socioeconomic status, or ability—have equal access to information. When algorithms systematically exclude certain voices, they betray this mission.

3. Legal and Ethical Risks

Many libraries operate under public funding, meaning they must comply with anti-discrimination laws (e.g., Title VI of the Civil Rights Act, Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act). If an algorithm disproportionately harms a protected group, the institution could face legal challenges.

4. Erosion of User Trust

When users feel their needs are ignored by biased systems, they lose confidence in the library’s services. A 2023 survey by the Library Research Round Table (LRRT) found that 47% of students would switch to alternative resources if they perceived a library’s digital tools as unfair.

Auraria Library recognizes these risks and has actively worked to dismantle bias in its systems. Their approach combines technical audits, policy changes, and community engagement—a model other institutions should emulate.

8 Actionable Strategies to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Auraria Library’s multi-pronged strategy to combat bias serves as a blueprint for other academic institutions. Below are eight key steps they’ve implemented, along with how other libraries can apply them.

1. Conduct Regular Bias Audits of Search & Recommendation Algorithms

What Auraria Does: Auraria Library partners with data scientists and library technologists to audit their search algorithms (e.g., EBSCO Discovery Service, WorldCat, and institutional repositories). They track query results to ensure that:

  • Diverse authors appear in top search results.
  • Marginalized topics (e.g., LGBTQ+ studies, Indigenous scholarship) are not demoted due to lower citation counts.
  • Non-English resources are properly indexed for multilingual users.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Use bias detection tools like IBM’s AI Fairness 360 or Google’s What-If Tool to analyze search behavior.
  • Benchmark against real-world usage data—if a certain demographic consistently finds fewer results, the algorithm may be biased.
  • Publish audit reports transparently to hold the institution accountable.

Real-World Example: At University of Michigan’s Clements Library, an audit revealed that searches for "African American history" frequently returned fewer results than "American history." After recalibrating keyword weighting, the library saw a 35% increase in relevant African American-focused resources in top search results.

2. Diversify Training Data for AI & Machine Learning Models

What Auraria Does: Auraria’s recommendation engines (e.g., LibGuides, Koha’s AI features) are trained on diverse datasets that include:

  • Books by underrepresented authors (e.g., Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, Ocean Vuong).
  • Scholarly works from global perspectives (e.g., decolonized history, postcolonial literature).
  • Multilingual metadata to improve accessibility for ESL (English as a Second Language) learners.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Curate a "bias mitigation dataset" with intentional inclusion of marginalized voices.
  • Collaborate with faculty to identify high-impact but underrepresented works.
  • Use tools like Zotero or Mendeley to tag and prioritize diverse sources in recommendation algorithms.

Real-World Example: Harvard’s Houghton Library faced criticism for its AI-powered digital collections tool, which prioritized rare books by white male scholars. After expanding training data to include women’s manuscripts and non-Western texts, the tool now displays a 50/50 split between historically marginalized and mainstream works in recommendations.

3. Implement Fairness Metrics in Algorithm Design

What Auraria Does: Auraria’s library technologists use fairness metrics (e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds) to ensure that:

  • Search results are not skewed by author race, gender, or institution affiliation.
  • Recommendations are not biased toward high-citation but homogeneous works.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Define fairness thresholds (e.g., "No more than 20% disparity in search results by demographic").
  • Use A/B testing to compare biased vs. unbiased algorithms in real-world scenarios.
  • Document metrics in library policy documents to ensure accountability.

Real-World Example: Stanford University Libraries introduced fairness scoring for their article recommendation system. Before the change, STEM papers by women were 25% less likely to appear in top suggestions. After adjusting citation weighting, the disparity dropped to 5%, improving gender equity in research exposure.

4. Provide Transparent Algorithm Explanations to Users

What Auraria Does: Auraria offers "How Our Recommendations Work" guides that explain:

  • How search algorithms rank results (e.g., "We prioritize relevance, not just popularity").
  • Why certain books may appear lower (e.g., "Newer works may take time to index").
  • How users can refine searches to find diverse perspectives.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Create a "Library AI Transparency Page" explaining algorithmic decisions.
  • Use tooltips or pop-ups to clarify how recommendations are generated.
  • Offer workshops on critical information literacy, teaching users to spot and mitigate bias.

Real-World Example: MIT Libraries launched "Algorithmic Literacy Workshops" where students learned to evaluate search results for bias. After the program, 82% of participants reported greater awareness of algorithmic fairness in their research.

5. Partner with Marginalized Communities to Shape Algorithm Design

What Auraria Does: Auraria’s Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) Committee includes:

  • Faculty from underrepresented backgrounds.
  • Student activists who test algorithms for bias.
  • Local community leaders (e.g., Indigenous scholars, LGBTQ+ organizations) to ensure culturally relevant content is prioritized.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Form a "Bias Review Board" with diverse stakeholders.
  • Host "Algorithm Design Hacksathons" where community members test and suggest improvements.
  • Publish community feedback in annual DEI reports.

Real-World Example: University of California, Berkeley’s Doe Library partnered with Black student organizations to audit their database recommendations. The students found that African American literature was often buried under mainstream white authors. After adjusting keyword prominence, the library saw a 40% increase in Black-authored books in top search results.

6. Use Bias-Aware Metadata Tagging

What Auraria Does: Auraria’s cataloging team now explicitly tags metadata to:

  • Highlight authorship diversity (e.g., "Author: Woman of Color").
  • Flag decolonized or intersectional scholarship.
  • Include alt-text descriptions for visually impaired users to ensure inclusive searchability.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Train catalogers in bias-aware metadata practices.
  • Use controlled vocabularies (e.g., LCNAF, RDA) to standardize diversity tags.
  • Audit existing records for implicit bias (e.g., gendered language in subject headings).

Real-World Example: New York Public Library (NYPL) updated its catalog to include "Identity-Based Tags" (e.g., "Queer Studies," "Latinx Literature"). This change doubled the visibility of LGBTQ+ and Latinx authors in search results.

7. Develop Bias Mitigation Policies & Training

What Auraria Does: Auraria has:

  • A formal "Algorithmic Fairness Policy" outlining bias detection and correction protocols.
  • Mandatory training for librarians, technologists, and catalogers on fair algorithm design.
  • An annual "Bias Audit Day" where all systems are re-evaluated for fairness.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Draft a policy with clear bias mitigation goals (e.g., "No algorithm will exclude more than 15% of demographic groups").
  • Offer certifications in algorithmic fairness for staff.
  • Conduct quarterly bias checks on high-traffic systems.

Real-World Example: University of Washington Libraries implemented "Fairness in Tech" training for all IT staff. After one year, search bias dropped by 30%, and user complaints about exclusionary recommendations fell by 45%.

8. Advocate for Open-Source & Bias-Resistant Tools

What Auraria Does: Auraria prefers open-source library systems (e.g., Koha, Evergreen) that allow greater transparency and customization for bias mitigation. They also:

  • Avoid proprietary black-box algorithms (e.g., some commercial discovery services).
  • Push for industry standards in fair algorithm design.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Research open-source alternatives to proprietary discovery tools.
  • Lobby vendors for bias auditing features.
  • Share best practices with other institutions via librarian networks (e.g., ALA, CLA).

Real-World Example: University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign switched from a proprietary discovery service to Koha, allowing them to customize fairness metrics. Within six months, search results became 60% more inclusive for minority authors.

Real-World Examples of Algorithmic Bias in Libraries (And How Auraria Fixed Them)

Auraria Library has documented several cases where bias was identified and corrected. Below are three key examples of how they detected and mitigated problematic algorithms.

Example 1: The "Whitewashing" of History Searches

The Problem: When students searched "American history" in Auraria’s database, results overwhelmingly featured white male historians. Scholarship by women, people of color, and Indigenous researchers was buried on later pages.

The Fix:

  • Auraria’s data team analyzed search query logs and found that keywords like "settler colonialism" and "decolonization" were underweighted in the algorithm.
  • They adjusted keyword prominence and added "diversity filters" (e.g., "Show only works by Indigenous authors").
  • Result: Within three months, 40% more diverse historical works appeared in top search results.

Lesson for Other Libraries:

  • Keyword weighting is a major source of bias—audit and balance search terms.
  • Allow user filters to override algorithmic defaults.

Example 2: The "STEM Gender Gap" in Recommendations

The Problem: Auraria’s article recommendation engine favored male researchers in STEM fields, leading to fewer citations for women scientists in student papers.

The Fix:

  • Auraria partnered with a STEM faculty DEI committee to identify high-impact women researchers.
  • They manually boosted citations for underrepresented scholars in the algorithm.
  • Result: Female STEM authors now appear in 30% of top recommendations, up from 12%.

Lesson for Other Libraries:

  • Field-specific bias exists—collaborate with faculty to correct disciplinary gaps.
  • Manual overrides can bridge algorithmic gaps while long-term fixes are implemented.

Example 3: The "Language Barrier" in Multilingual Searches

The Problem: Non-English speakers at Auraria struggled to find resources because the search algorithm prioritized English-language metadata, demoting bilingual and non-English works.

The Fix:

  • Auraria added "language affinity" scoring, prioritizing multilingual resources for non-English speakers.
  • They partnered with ESL tutors to test search usability for international students.
  • Result: Spanish-language resources now appear in 25% of non-English searches, up from 5%.

Lesson for Other Libraries:

  • Multilingual users are often excluded—design algorithms with linguistic diversity in mind.
