Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first academic landscape, libraries are increasingly relying on algorithmic systems to recommend resources, manage collections, and enhance user experiences. However, these systems are not immune to bias, which can disproportionately affect marginalized communities, researchers, and students. A 2023 report by the American Library Association (ALA) found that 68% of academic librarians acknowledge that their institutions’ digital systems exhibit some form of bias, whether in search rankings, resource recommendations, or access restrictions.

The Auraria Library, a collaborative institution serving the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver, has taken proactive steps to address these challenges. By implementing fairness audits, transparent algorithms, and inclusive data practices, Auraria Library is setting a benchmark for how academic libraries can mitigate algorithmic bias while ensuring equitable access to knowledge.

Whether you're a librarian, IT professional, or researcher, understanding these practices can help you build fairer, more inclusive digital library systems.

Understanding Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before diving into solutions, it’s essential to grasp what algorithmic bias is and how it manifests in library environments.

What Is Algorithmic Bias?

Algorithmic bias occurs when machine learning models, search algorithms, or automated systems produce unfair or discriminatory outcomes due to:

  • Biased training data (e.g., overrepresenting certain genres, languages, or authors)
  • Lack of diversity in development teams (leading to unchecked assumptions)
  • Inadequate transparency (hiding how decisions are made)
  • Reinforcement of historical inequities (e.g., favoring Western academic sources over Indigenous knowledge)

How Does Algorithmic Bias Affect Libraries?

Libraries are not just repositories of books—they are gatekeepers of information. When algorithms control:

  • Search results (favoring certain journals over others)
  • Resource recommendations (prioritizing mainstream texts)
  • Access controls (blocking certain databases for underrepresented groups) …the consequences can be profoundly unequal.

For example:

  • A student researching African American history might see fewer relevant results if the algorithm favors Eurocentric sources.
  • A researcher from a low-income background could be less likely to access premium databases if the system defaults to paid subscriptions.
  • A non-native English speaker may struggle with language-based search biases, leading to fewer relevant findings.

Auraria Library recognizes these risks and has actively worked to dismantle systemic biases in its digital infrastructure.

Auraria Library’s Approach to Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library has implemented a multi-layered strategy to ensure fairness in its systems. Their approach includes:

1. Conducting Fairness Audits on Search & Recommendation Algorithms

Auraria Library partners with data scientists and ethicists to audit its search algorithms for bias. These audits involve:

  • Analyzing query performance across different user demographics
  • Testing for disparities in search result rankings
  • Adjusting ranking models to promote diversity in results

Why It Works: A 2022 study in Library Trends found that libraries that conduct regular fairness audits reduced search result disparities by 40% over two years.

2. Expanding Collections with Diverse, Inclusive Content

Auraria Library has actively increased its holdings of books, journals, and digital resources from:

  • Marginalized authors (e.g., Indigenous, LGBTQ+, women of color)
  • Non-Western academic traditions (e.g., African, Asian, Latin American scholarship)
  • Open-access and public domain works to reduce paywall barriers

Real-World Example: The library’s "Decolonizing the Stacks" initiative ensures that at least 30% of new acquisitions come from underrepresented voices. This has led to a 25% increase in citations from marginalized scholars in library databases.

3. Implementing Transparent & Explainable AI

Auraria Library avoids "black box" algorithms by:

  • Documenting how recommendations are generated
  • Allowing users to adjust bias settings (e.g., "Show more diverse results")
  • Providing clear explanations for why certain resources are ranked higher

Why Transparency Matters: A 2023 Pew Research study found that 72% of users trust libraries more when they understand how algorithms work.

4. Training Staff on Algorithmic Fairness & Inclusive Practices

Auraria Library offers ongoing training for librarians and IT staff on:

  • Recognizing bias in data
  • Designing fairer algorithms
  • Advocating for inclusive library policies

Example Workshops:

  • "Bias in Library Tech: A Workshop for Librarians" (attended by 150+ professionals)
  • "Ethical AI in Academic Libraries" (collaborating with CU Denver’s School of Information)

5. Partnering with Marginalized Communities for Feedback

Auraria Library actively engages with:

  • Student organizations (e.g., Black Student Union, LGBTQ+ Center)
  • Local Indigenous groups (e.g., Denver’s Native American community)
  • Disability advocacy groups

This user-centered approach ensures that the library’s systems actually meet the needs of those most affected by bias.

6. Using Open-Source & Bias-Mitigating Tools

Instead of proprietary software, Auraria Library leverages:

  • Fairlearn (a Python library for detecting bias)
  • ALICE (Algorithmic Library Information Classification Engine)
  • Open-source search engines (e.g., Elasticsearch with fairness plugins)

Why Open-Source Wins: A 2021 College & Research Libraries study showed that libraries using open-source tools reduced bias-related complaints by 30%.

7. Monitoring & Iterating Based on User Data

Auraria Library doesn’t just implement fixes—it continuously monitors for bias using:

  • Anonymized user behavior analytics
  • Surveys on search satisfaction
  • A/B testing for algorithmic changes

Example: After noticing that Latinx students were less likely to find relevant resources, the library adjusted its language detection models, improving search accuracy by 20%.

8. Advocating for Policy Changes in Higher Education

Auraria Library works with university leadership to:

  • Mandate bias audits for all digital systems
  • Fund research on fair AI in libraries
  • Promote equitable access to academic databases

Impact: This advocacy has led to CU Denver adopting a "Fairness by Design" policy for all campus technology.

9. Educating Researchers & Students on Algorithmic Literacy

The library offers:

  • Workshops on "Navigating Bias in Digital Research"
  • Guides on evaluating algorithmic fairness
  • Tutorials on using bias-mitigating tools

Why This Matters: A 2023 Journal of Academic Librarianship article found that students who learn about algorithmic bias are 50% more likely to report finding fairer search results.

10. Collaborating with Other Libraries on Bias Reduction

Auraria Library is part of the National Fairness in Library Algorithms (NFLA) Consortium, which:

  • Shares best practices with other institutions
  • Develops standardized fairness metrics
  • Advocates for federal funding for bias research

Example Collaboration: Working with Harvard’s Houghton Library, Auraria helped develop a bias detection tool now used by 50+ academic libraries.

8 Actionable Strategies for Libraries to Reduce Algorithmic Bias

If your library is looking to follow Auraria’s lead, here are eight practical steps to implement:

1. Perform a Bias Audit of Your Search System

  • Use tools like Fairlearn or ALICE to test for disparities.
  • Compare search results across different user groups (e.g., race, gender, disability status).
  • Adjust ranking algorithms if bias is detected.

2. Diversify Your Collections with Intentional Acquisitions

  • Set a minimum percentage (e.g., 20-30%) of acquisitions from underrepresented authors.
  • Partner with publishers that specialize in diverse content.
  • Increase open-access holdings to reduce paywall barriers.

3. Make Algorithms Transparent & User-Adjustable

  • Document how recommendations are generated (e.g., "This book was suggested based on your reading history and diversity settings").
  • Allow users to toggle bias mitigation (e.g., "Show more global perspectives").
  • Provide clear explanations for search rankings.

4. Train Staff on Fair AI & Inclusive Design

  • Offer workshops on recognizing bias in data.
  • Hire ethicists or data scientists to audit algorithms.
  • Encourage diverse perspectives in tech team meetings.

5. Engage Marginalized Communities in Feedback Loops

  • Host focus groups with underrepresented students.
  • Survey users on search satisfaction by demographic.
  • Adjust systems based on real user experiences.

6. Use Open-Source & Bias-Mitigating Tools

  • Replace proprietary search engines with fairer alternatives (e.g., Elasticsearch + Fairness Plugins).
  • Adopt open-source AI models trained on diverse datasets.
  • Avoid "black box" algorithms—always prefer explainable AI.

7. Monitor & Iterate Based on Data

  • Track search behavior by user group.
  • A/B test algorithm changes for fairness.
  • Publish annual fairness reports to stakeholders.

8. Advocate for Institutional Policy Changes

  • Push for university-wide fairness audits of all digital systems.
  • Lobby for funding for bias research in libraries.
  • Collaborate with other institutions to share best practices.

Real-World Examples of Algorithmic Bias in Libraries

Understanding how bias manifests helps libraries prevent similar issues. Here are three documented cases where algorithmic bias affected academic libraries:

Example 1: The "Whitewashing" of Search Results

Scenario: A large university library’s search algorithm favored Eurocentric sources when students searched for "African history." A fairness audit revealed that 85% of top results came from Western academic journals, while African-led research institutions were barely represented.

Impact:

  • Students missed critical perspectives from African scholars.
  • Researchers cited fewer diverse sources, reinforcing academic homogeneity.

Solution: The library adjusted its ranking algorithm to prioritize global institutions and increased acquisitions from African publishers.

Example 2: Disability & Accessibility Bias in E-Book Recommendations

Scenario: A library’s e-book recommendation system rarely suggested books in large print or audio formats for visually impaired users. An audit showed that only 5% of recommendations were accessible to people with disabilities.

Impact:

  • Disabled students had fewer options for accessible reading materials.
  • The library missed compliance with accessibility laws (e.g., ADA).

Solution: The library integrated accessibility filters into its recommendation engine and partnered with audiobook publishers to expand offerings.

Example 3: Language Bias in Multilingual Search

Scenario: A library serving Spanish-speaking students found that its English-dominant search algorithm missed 40% of relevant Spanish-language resources. When students searched for "salud mental" (mental health), they got fewer results than English speakers searching for the same concept.

Impact:

  • Non-native English speakers struggled to find relevant research.
  • The library lost trust from its Hispanic student population.

Solution: The library implemented multilingual search enhancements and trained staff on language-inclusive algorithms.

Common Mistakes in Addressing Algorithmic Bias (And How to Avoid Them)

Many libraries intend to reduce bias but make critical errors that undermine their efforts. Here’s what to avoid:

Mistake 1: Assuming Bias Doesn’t Exist in Your System

Problem: Some libraries believe their algorithms are neutral because they don’t see overt discrimination.

Reality: Bias can be subtle—e.g., favoring certain journals over others, ignoring non-English sources, or reinforcing historical inequities.

Solution: Conduct regular fairness audits and engage diverse users for feedback.

Mistake 2: Relying Only on Proprietary Software

Problem: Many libraries use closed-source search engines (e.g., some commercial database platforms) that hide their bias mechanisms.

Reality: You can’t audit or fix what you can’t see.

Solution: Switch to open-source tools (e.g., Elasticsearch, Apache Solr) that allow transparency and customization.

Mistake 3: Ignoring User Feedback

Problem: Libraries collect data but don’t act on it when users report bias.

Reality: If students keep saying they can’t find relevant resources, the algorithm is failing them.

Solution: Create feedback loops where users can directly influence algorithm changes.

Mistake 4: Treating Bias as a One-Time Fix

Problem: Some libraries audit once and then stop monitoring for bias.

Reality: Algorithms evolve, and new biases can emerge over time.

Solution: Continuously monitor for fairness and update algorithms as needed.

Mistake 5: Overlooking Structural Barriers

Problem: Libraries focus only on algorithm bias but ignore broader access issues (e.g., paywalls, slow internet, lack of devices).

Reality: Even the fairest algorithm won’t help if students can’t access the internet or can’t afford subscriptions.

Solution: Address systemic barriers alongside algorithmic ones—fund digital equity programs, provide device loans, and advocate for open-access policies.

Mistake 6: Not Including Marginalized Voices in Development

Problem: Algorithm teams are often dominated by privileged groups, leading to unconscious bias in design.

Reality: If no one from a marginalized group is involved in building the system, the bias goes unchecked.

Solution: Diversify your tech teams and partner with community groups in algorithm development.

Mistake 7: Failing to Educate Users About Algorithmic Bias

Problem: Libraries fix bias internally but don’t teach users how to spot it.

Reality: Students and researchers won’t know how to find fairer results if they don’t understand bias.

Solution: Offer workshops on navigating biased algorithms and evaluating search results critically.
