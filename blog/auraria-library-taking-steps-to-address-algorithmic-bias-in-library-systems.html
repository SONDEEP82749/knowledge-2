Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first academic landscape, libraries are increasingly relying on algorithmic systems to enhance user experience, recommend resources, and streamline access to information. However, these systems are not immune to algorithmic bias—a phenomenon where automated processes disproportionately favor or disadvantage certain groups based on factors like race, gender, socioeconomic status, or geographic location.

A 2023 study by the American Library Association (ALA) found that 68% of academic librarians report concerns about bias in library automation tools, with 42% citing racial and ethnic disparities in search results. Meanwhile, a 2024 report from the University of Colorado Denver’s Auraria Library revealed that 37% of students from underrepresented backgrounds struggle to find relevant resources due to flawed recommendation algorithms.

Auraria Library, a collaborative institution serving the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver, has taken proactive steps to address these issues. By implementing fairness audits, diverse data curation, and transparent algorithmic governance, Auraria Library is setting a national benchmark for how academic libraries can mitigate bias in digital systems.

Why Algorithmic Bias in Libraries Is a Critical Issue

Libraries are gatekeepers of knowledge, and when algorithms systematically exclude or marginalize certain groups, they reinforce digital inequality. Here’s why this is a pressing concern:

1. Reinforcing Historical Exclusions

Many academic libraries were historically exclusionary, with limited access for marginalized communities. While physical collections have improved, digital algorithms can perpetuate these biases by:

  • Over-indexing resources from elite institutions
  • Underscoring works by authors from underrepresented backgrounds
  • Favoring certain genres, topics, or languages over others

A 2023 Pew Research Center study found that only 12% of top search results in academic databases were authored by women, while only 8% featured authors of color.

2. Impact on Student Success

Students from low-income, first-generation, or minority backgrounds rely heavily on library resources for research and education. When algorithms fail to recommend relevant materials, they face:

  • Higher academic stress due to difficulty finding sources
  • Reduced confidence in their ability to succeed
  • Lower engagement with digital library services

Auraria Library’s 2024 equity report noted that students who identified as Black, Indigenous, or people of color (BIPOC) were 2.3 times more likely to report frustration with search results compared to their peers.

3. Legal and Ethical Risks

Libraries operate under public trust and legal obligations to provide equitable access. Failure to address bias can lead to:

  • Lawsuits (e.g., claims of disparate impact under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act)
  • Loss of institutional reputation
  • Violation of open access principles

The American Library Association’s Code of Ethics explicitly states that librarians must "provide access to information to all people"—a principle that algorithmic bias directly undermines.

Auraria Library’s 8-Step Approach to Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library has developed a multi-layered strategy to ensure fairness in its digital systems. Below are the eight key steps they’ve implemented, along with practical takeaways for other institutions.

1. Conducting Fairness Audits of Existing Algorithms

What Auraria Library Did: Before making any changes, Auraria Library audited its existing recommendation engines (used in the library’s catalog, research databases, and digital collections). They partnered with data scientists from the University of Colorado Denver’s School of Public Affairs to analyze:

  • Search result rankings for queries related to social justice, STEM, and humanities
  • Author and publisher representation in top recommendations
  • User engagement metrics (e.g., clicks, downloads) by demographic group

Key Findings:

  • White male authors dominated 70% of top recommendations in STEM fields.
  • Books by Black authors were 30% less likely to appear in the first 10 search results for social sciences queries.
  • Non-English language resources were systematically deprioritized in multilingual searches.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Hire external auditors (or partner with university researchers) to assess bias.
  • Use open-source fairness tools like IBM’s AI Fairness 360 or Google’s What-If Tool.
  • Publish audit reports transparently to build trust with users.

2. Diversifying Data Sources for Training Algorithms

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria realized that their algorithms were trained primarily on data from elite institutions, leading to biased recommendations. To fix this, they:

  • Expanded their training data to include:
    • Open-access repositories (e.g., DOAB, Directory of Open Access Books)
    • Indigenous knowledge databases (e.g., Native Land Digital)
    • Scholarly works from Global South institutions (e.g., African Journals Online)
  • Incorporated user behavior data from underrepresented groups to ensure algorithms reflected diverse research interests.

Real-World Impact: After updating its data sources, Auraria saw a 40% increase in recommendations for Black and Indigenous authors in social sciences and humanities.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Partner with global academic networks (e.g., WorldCat, COUNTER) to access diverse datasets.
  • Encourage faculty and students to submit underserved collections for algorithmic training.
  • Use weighted sampling to ensure marginalized voices are proportionally represented.

3. Implementing Bias Mitigation Techniques in Search Algorithms

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library worked with its IT and data science teams to implement three key bias-mitigation strategies:

  1. Re-ranking Algorithms – After initial search results, the system re-ranks items to ensure diverse representation without sacrificing relevance.
  2. Fairness Constraints – The algorithm was programmed to penalize rankings that excluded certain demographics beyond a statistically significant threshold.
  3. Explicit Diversity Prompts – For queries related to social justice, history, or cultural studies, the system prioritized works by marginalized authors unless the user explicitly requested otherwise.

Example: When a student searched "Black feminist theory," the algorithm no longer defaulted to works by white male scholars but instead featured works by Angela Davis, Audre Lorde, and bell hooks in the top 5 results.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Work with developers to integrate fairness-aware ranking models.
  • Test A/B variations of search algorithms to measure bias reduction.
  • Allow user opt-ins/opt-outs for diversity-focused recommendations.

4. Training Staff on Algorithmic Bias Awareness

What Auraria Library Did: Recognizing that human librarians play a critical role in shaping algorithmic fairness, Auraria implemented:

  • Mandatory workshops on algorithmic bias, data ethics, and inclusive librarianship.
  • Case studies on real-world algorithmic failures (e.g., Amazon’s racist hiring algorithm, Google’s biased image search).
  • Peer review processes where librarians audit algorithmic decisions before implementation.

Key Takeaway: A 2024 survey by the Association of College & Research Libraries (ACRL) found that librarians who received bias training were 3.2 times more likely to identify and address algorithmic issues in their systems.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Develop in-house training modules (or use resources from ALA, ACRL, or Data & Society Research Institute).
  • Invite external speakers (e.g., data ethicists, civil rights advocates) to discuss bias in tech.
  • Create a "Bias Response Team" of librarians who specialize in algorithmic fairness.

5. Engaging Users in Co-Designing Fair Algorithms

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library recognized that users—especially marginalized groups—have unique insights into what constitutes a fair algorithm. They:

  • Hosted focus groups with BIPOC students, LGBTQ+ researchers, and first-generation college students to gather feedback on search experiences.
  • Launched a "Fairness Feedback Portal" where users could report biased results and suggest improvements.
  • Included diversity questions in user surveys, asking:
    • "Do you feel the library’s search results reflect your research needs?"
    • "Have you ever struggled to find resources by authors from your background?"

Result: The feedback led to adjustments in recommendation algorithms, including increased visibility for Indigenous and queer studies resources.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Conduct participatory design sessions with diverse user groups.
  • Use sentiment analysis tools to gauge user frustration with biased systems.
  • Publish transparency reports showing how user feedback influences algorithmic changes.

6. Advocating for Open Standards in Library Technology

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria recognized that proprietary library software (e.g., Ex Libris, OCLC) often lacks transparency in algorithmic decision-making. To combat this, they:

  • Pushed for open-source alternatives like Koha, Evergreen, or Inmagic where algorithms can be audited and modified.
  • Lobbied for industry standards (e.g., FAIR Data Principles, AI Ethics Guidelines) in library tech vendors.
  • Collaborated with other institutions (e.g., University of Michigan, MIT Libraries) to develop collective fairness benchmarks.

Real-World Example: When Auraria’s OCLC WorldCat search was found to favor English-language resources, they negotiated with OCLC to implement multilingual fairness adjustments in their API.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Research open-source library systems that allow algorithm transparency.
  • Join advocacy groups like Libraries Transform or Code4Lib to push for ethical tech standards.
  • Demand fairness audits from vendors before purchasing new systems.

7. Monitoring and Iterating Based on Real-World Data

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library didn’t treat bias mitigation as a one-time fix—instead, they continuously monitored their systems using:

  • Demographic-tagged analytics (tracking user groups who interact with recommendations).
  • A/B testing to compare biased vs. fair algorithm versions.
  • Quarterly fairness audits to detect new biases as data changes.

Example: After implementing fairness constraints, Auraria noticed that STEM recommendations still favored male authors. They adjusted the algorithm to include more female and non-binary researchers in top results.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Set up automated bias detection using machine learning models.
  • Schedule regular fairness reviews (e.g., biannually).
  • Use dashboards to track demographic engagement metrics.

8. Educating the Community on Algorithmic Fairness

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria understood that users needed to understand why algorithms sometimes "failed" them. They:

  • Created a "Bias in Algorithms" guide for students, faculty, and staff.
  • Hosted public forums with data scientists and ethicists to discuss bias.
  • Developed a "How to Spot Algorithmic Bias" workshop for researchers.

Impact: A 2024 student survey showed that 72% of respondents felt more informed about algorithmic fairness after Auraria’s educational initiatives.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Develop plain-language guides on algorithmic bias.
  • Offer workshops on critical information literacy (e.g., how to evaluate biased search results).
  • Partner with media centers to produce videos or podcasts on the topic.

Real-World Examples of Algorithmic Bias in Libraries (and How They Were Fixed)

Example 1: The "Whitewashing" of Historical Search Results

Issue: A major university library’s historical document search (using OCR technology) frequently misclassified Black and Indigenous authors as "unknown" or "unidentified," leading to fewer results for their work.

Solution: The library:

  1. Partnered with historians to retrain OCR models on diverse historical texts.
  2. Added manual verification steps for high-bias queries.
  3. Published a correction guide for users.

Result: Searches for "Harriet Tubman" or "Chief Seattle" now consistently returned accurate results within the top 3.

Lesson: OCR and automated classification systems can reinforce historical erasure—libraries must audit and retrain these tools.

Example 2: The "Gender Gap" in STEM Recommendations

Issue: A research university’s STEM database overrepresented male authors by 60% in top recommendations, despite equal publication rates for female researchers.

Solution: The library:

  1. Analyzed citation networks to identify undercited female researchers.
  2. Adjusted recommendation weights to prioritize diverse voices without sacrificing relevance.
  3. Featured "Hidden Figures" collections to highlight marginalized contributors.

Result: Within six months, female-authored STEM papers appeared in the top 5 results 40% more often.

Lesson: Citation bias is systemic—libraries must actively correct for it in recommendation systems.

Example 3: The "Language Barrier" in Multilingual Searches

Issue: A public library’s multilingual search tool prioritized English-language results, making it difficult for Spanish-speaking users to find bilingual or Spanish-only resources.

Solution: The library:

  1. Implemented language-aware ranking (e.g., Spanish queries returned more Spanish results).
  2. Added a "Diversity Filter" to surface non-English materials.
  3. Partnered with local Hispanic cultural centers to curate relevant collections.

Result: Spanish-speaking users reported a 50% improvement in finding culturally relevant materials.

Lesson: Multilingual algorithms must be "language-aware"—defaulting to English excludes non-native speakers.
