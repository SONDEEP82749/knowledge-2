Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first academic landscape, libraries are increasingly relying on algorithmic systems to improve user experience, recommend resources, and streamline access to information. However, these systems—while powerful—are not immune to algorithmic bias, a phenomenon where automated processes unfairly favor or disadvantage certain groups based on factors like race, gender, socioeconomic status, or geographic location.

A recent 2023 report by the American Library Association (ALA) found that 68% of academic librarians acknowledge bias in their institution’s digital systems, yet only 32% have implemented formal strategies to mitigate it. Meanwhile, a 2024 study published in Library Trends revealed that 45% of library users—particularly underrepresented students—experience disproportionate access barriers due to biased recommendation algorithms.

One institution at the forefront of addressing this issue is the Auraria Library, a collaborative research library serving the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver. Recognizing the ethical and practical implications of algorithmic bias, Auraria Library has taken proactive steps to audit, refine, and democratize its digital systems.

This blog post explores how Auraria Library is tackling algorithmic bias, the key strategies libraries can adopt, real-world examples of bias in action, and actionable steps other institutions can take to ensure fair and equitable access to information.

Understanding Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before diving into solutions, it’s essential to understand what algorithmic bias looks like in a library context and why it matters.

What Is Algorithmic Bias?

Algorithmic bias occurs when machine learning models, recommendation engines, or automated classification systems produce outcomes that disproportionately disadvantage certain groups. In libraries, this bias can manifest in several ways:

  1. Recommendation Bias – Certain books, journals, or research topics are overrepresented or underrepresented based on user demographics.
  2. Access Bias – Some users face higher friction in discovering resources due to algorithmic gatekeeping (e.g., search results favoring elite institutions).
  3. Classification Bias – Subject categorization systems may mislabel or exclude works by marginalized authors or underrepresented topics.
  4. Discovery Bias – Search algorithms may rank certain sources higher based on historical usage patterns, reinforcing existing inequalities.

Why Does Algorithmic Bias Matter in Libraries?

Libraries are public institutions with a moral obligation to provide equitable access to knowledge. When algorithms perpetuate bias:

  • Underrepresented students may struggle to find relevant research materials.
  • Marginalized voices in literature, history, and academia may be systematically deprioritized.
  • Trust in digital systems erodes when users feel their needs are being ignored.

Auraria Library’s approach to combating bias is not just about technical fixes—it’s about ethical design, transparency, and community engagement.

Auraria Library’s Approach to Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library has implemented a multi-layered strategy to address bias in its systems. Their approach combines technical audits, user feedback, and policy changes. Below are the key initiatives they’ve undertaken:

1. Conducting Bias Audits on Recommendation Systems

Auraria Library partnered with data scientists and librarians to analyze its digital recommendation engine, which suggests books, articles, and databases to users.

How They Did It:

  • Historical Data Analysis – They examined three years of user interaction data to identify patterns where certain demographics (e.g., STEM students vs. humanities students) received different recommendations.
  • Diversity Metrics – They tracked whether books by women, authors of color, or non-Western scholars were being under-recommended compared to majority-authored works.
  • A/B Testing – They ran controlled experiments where the algorithm was adjusted to increase diversity in suggestions, measuring user engagement before and after.

Result: They found that STEM students were 30% more likely to receive recommendations from a narrower set of elite journals, while humanities students faced longer discovery paths for interdisciplinary research.

Lesson for Other Libraries:

  • Regularly audit recommendation systems using diversity metrics.
  • Involve librarians and data ethicists in the analysis to avoid confirmation bias.

2. Implementing Fairness Constraints in Search Algorithms

Auraria Library’s online catalog and discovery tool (a modified version of Koha) was found to favor recently accessed items, creating a "rich-get-richer" effect where popular books dominated search results.

Their Solution:

  • FairRank Algorithm – They integrated a fairness-aware ranking system that penalizes over-representation of certain items while boosting underrepresented ones.
  • Topic Diversity Checks – The system now ensures that search results include at least 20% works by marginalized authors when relevant.

Real-World Impact: A 2023 user study showed that after implementing these changes, students from low-income backgrounds were 25% more likely to find relevant course materials in their first search attempt.

Key Takeaway:

  • Search algorithms should not just optimize for relevance—they must optimize for fairness.
  • Set explicit diversity goals in your ranking criteria.

3. Expanding Metadata Standards to Reduce Classification Bias

Traditional Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH) and Dewey Decimal classifications have been criticized for reinforcing colonial and Eurocentric biases. Auraria Library took steps to modernize their metadata to better reflect diverse perspectives.

Actions Taken:

  • Adopted Linked Open Data (LOD) Standards – They integrated Wikidata and VIAF (Virtual International Authority File) to cross-reference marginalized authors with their works.
  • Added Bias-Resistant Tags – They implemented custom metadata fields to flag historically excluded voices (e.g., Indigenous scholars, LGBTQ+ authors).
  • Trained Staff on Inclusive Cataloging – Librarians underwent workshops on decolonizing metadata, learning to avoid biased descriptors (e.g., replacing "Native American" with "Indigenous Peoples").

Example of Bias in Action: Before the changes, a search for "African American history" would prioritize works by white scholars on Black history. After updates, the system surface-authored works by Black historians alongside mainstream texts.

Why This Matters:

  • Metadata is the foundation of discovery—if it’s biased, everything else is too.
  • Small changes in labeling can drastically improve representation.

4. Creating Bias Mitigation Guidelines for Vendors

Many libraries rely on third-party discovery tools (e.g., Ex Libris Alma, OCLC WorldShare). Auraria Library recognized that vendor algorithms could introduce bias, so they negotiated fairness clauses in their contracts.

Their Contract Requirements:

  • Transparency Reports – Vendors must disclose how their algorithms rank items and whether they track user demographics.
  • Bias Audits – Vendors must conduct annual fairness assessments and share results.
  • Diversity Benchmarks – Vendors must ensure that at least 30% of recommended items come from diverse publishers or authors.

Result: One vendor, after being held accountable, adjusted its algorithm to reduce over-representation of Ivy League journals in search results.

For Other Libraries:

  • Demand fairness clauses in vendor contracts.
  • Avoid "black box" systems—push for explainable AI in library tech.

5. Launching a User Feedback Loop for Algorithmic Fairness

Auraria Library implemented a continuous feedback system where users could report perceived bias in recommendations.

How It Works:

  • "Fairness Flag" – Users can tag recommendations as unfair or missing (e.g., "Why isn’t this book by a woman recommended?").
  • Automated Follow-Up – The system analyzes patterns in feedback and adjusts algorithms accordingly.
  • Public Transparency Report – Quarterly reports detail how feedback has influenced changes.

Example: A group of Latinx students reported that Latin American literature was hard to find. The feedback led to expanded metadata tags and better algorithmic weighting for those topics.

Why This Works:

  • Users are the best detectors of bias—they live it daily.
  • Feedback loops create accountability.

6. Developing Algorithmic Bias Training for Staff

Recognizing that librarians and tech staff play a crucial role in designing and maintaining digital systems, Auraria Library launched mandatory training on algorithmic fairness.

Training Modules Include:

  • How Bias Propagates in Algorithms – Case studies on Amazon’s hiring AI, Google’s search bias, and Netflix’s recommendation bias.
  • Ethical AI Design Principles – Lessons on fairness, transparency, and accountability.
  • Practical Auditing Tools – Training on using tools like IBM’s AI Fairness 360 to test their own systems.

Result: Staff now actively question whether new tech implementations could introduce bias before deployment.

For Other Libraries:

  • Train staff on algorithmic ethics—it’s not just a tech issue.
  • Make fairness a core competency in digital literacy programs.

7. Partnering with Marginalized Communities for Co-Design

Auraria Library took a community-centered approach, working directly with underrepresented student groups to co-design fairer systems.

How They Did It:

  • Student Advisory Boards – They formed groups with LGBTQ+, Indigenous, and first-gen students to review algorithmic outputs.
  • Workshops on "Algorithmic Literacy" – Students learned how to evaluate fairness in recommendations.
  • Collaborative Testing – Students tested prototypes of new algorithms and provided real-time feedback.

Impact: One student noted that after their input, the system started suggesting more "relevant" books—ones that reflected their lived experiences.

Key Lesson:

  • Libraries should not design for users—they should design with them.
  • Marginalized communities have the best insights into bias.

8. Advocating for Policy Changes in Higher Education

Auraria Library didn’t stop at internal fixes—they pushed for institutional policy changes to embed fairness into university-wide digital systems.

Their Advocacy Efforts:

  • Campus-Wide Algorithmic Bias Task Force – They convinced university leadership to form a committee to audit all automated systems (not just libraries).
  • Fairness in Admissions & Scholarships – They argued that AI-driven admissions tools (used by some departments) should undergo bias testing.
  • Open Data Policies – They advocated for mandatory transparency in how university data is used in algorithmic decisions.

Result: The university now requires all AI-driven systems to undergo fairness assessments before deployment.

For Other Institutions:

  • Libraries should be leaders in ethical AI advocacy.
  • Push for campus-wide policies—don’t let bias exist in silos.

9. Open-Sourcing Fairness Tools for the Library Community

Auraria Library didn’t keep their findings secret—they open-sourced some of their bias mitigation tools to help other libraries.

What They Released:

  • FairRank Algorithm Code – A modular ranking system that libraries can integrate into their discovery tools.
  • Metadata Bias Checker – A tool to audit catalog records for underrepresentation.
  • Feedback Loop Framework – A template for implementing user-reported bias tracking.

Why This Matters:

  • Collaboration accelerates progress—other libraries can build on their work.
  • Transparency builds trust in library tech.

10. Measuring Success: KPIs for Algorithmic Fairness

To ensure their efforts were effective, Auraria Library defined key performance indicators (KPIs) to track fairness.

Metrics They Track: ✅ Diversity in Recommendations – % of suggested items by marginalized authors. ✅ Discovery Time – How quickly underrepresented users find relevant materials. ✅ User Satisfaction Scores – Feedback on fairness perceptions. ✅ Bias Incident Reports – Number of user-reported unfair recommendations. ✅ Metadata Coverage – % of historically excluded topics properly tagged.

Example: After implementing these metrics, they found that discovery time for first-gen students dropped by 20% in the first year.

For Other Libraries:

  • Define fairness metrics—you can’t improve what you don’t measure.
  • Track progress over time—bias is not a one-time fix.

Common Mistakes in Addressing Algorithmic Bias (And How to Avoid Them)

While Auraria Library’s approach is a model for fairness, many institutions make critical errors when trying to combat bias. Here’s what not to do:

❌ Mistake 1: Treating Bias as a "Tech Problem" Only

Why It’s Wrong: Some libraries assume that fixing algorithms is enough, but bias is also a cultural and policy issue.

How to Avoid It:

  • Engage librarians, ethicists, and users in the process.
  • Address systemic biases in cataloging, funding, and collection development.

❌ Mistake 2: Relying on "One-Size-Fits-All" Solutions

Why It’s Wrong: What works for a large research university may not work for a community college with different user needs.

How to Avoid It:

  • Tailor fairness strategies to your institution’s specific demographics.
  • Pilot changes before full implementation.

❌ Mistake 3: Ignoring Vendor Algorithms

Why It’s Wrong: Many libraries don’t audit third-party tools, assuming they’re "neutral."

How to Avoid It:

  • Demand fairness clauses in vendor contracts.
  • Use open-source alternatives when possible.

❌ Mistake 4: Overlooking Metadata Bias

Why It’s Wrong: Even if recommendations are fair, biased metadata can undermine discovery.

How to Avoid It:

  • Regularly audit catalog records for exclusionary language.
  • Train staff on inclusive cataloging.

❌ Mistake 5: Not Measuring Impact

Why It’s Wrong: Without data, you can’t prove if your changes actually worked.

How to Avoid It:

  • Set clear KPIs for fairness.
  • Collect user feedback regularly.

❌ Mistake 6: Assuming Bias is "Fixed" After One Audit

Why It’s Wrong: Algorithms evolve, and new biases can emerge over time.

How to Avoid It:

  • Conduct regular bias audits (quarterly or annually).
  • Stay updated on new fairness research.
