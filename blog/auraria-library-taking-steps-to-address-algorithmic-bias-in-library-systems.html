Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In an era where technology drives nearly every aspect of information access, academic libraries—including institutions like the Auraria Library—are increasingly reliant on algorithms to recommend resources, organize collections, and personalize user experiences. However, these automated systems are not immune to bias. Research from the Pew Research Center (2022) found that 68% of Americans are concerned about algorithmic bias in digital platforms, and this skepticism extends to academic and public libraries.

The Auraria Library, a joint venture between the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver, has recognized this challenge. As a major research and teaching library serving diverse student populations, Auraria must ensure its systems do not perpetuate exclusionary practices. This blog post explores how Auraria Library is addressing algorithmic bias, the strategies they’re implementing, and actionable steps other libraries can take to foster fairness in their digital systems.

Understanding Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before diving into solutions, it’s essential to understand what algorithmic bias means in a library context. Unlike commercial platforms like social media or search engines, libraries operate under different ethical and legal frameworks. However, bias can still manifest in several ways:

  1. Overrepresentation or Underrepresentation of Topics – Algorithms may favor certain subjects (e.g., STEM over humanities) based on historical usage patterns.
  2. Disparities in Resource Recommendations – Users from marginalized backgrounds may receive fewer relevant suggestions due to skewed data.
  3. Accessibility Barriers – AI-driven interfaces may fail to accommodate users with disabilities, reinforcing systemic exclusion.
  4. Citation and Discovery Bias – Search algorithms might prioritize certain publishers or formats (e.g., peer-reviewed journals over open-access preprints), limiting equitable access.

A 2023 study by the American Library Association (ALA) highlighted that 47% of academic librarians reported concerns about algorithmic bias in their institutions, with many lacking formal training on how to mitigate it. Auraria Library is taking proactive steps to address these issues, setting a precedent for other institutions.

Auraria Library’s Approach to Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library has implemented a multi-faceted strategy to ensure fairness in its digital systems. Their approach combines technical adjustments, policy changes, and user-centered design. Below, we break down their key initiatives and how other libraries can apply similar principles.

8 Actionable Strategies to Reduce Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

1. Audit and Diversify Training Data

Problem: Many library algorithms rely on historical usage data, which often reflects past biases (e.g., favoring white male authors or Western perspectives).

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library conducts regular audits of its recommendation engines, ensuring that training datasets include a broad range of voices, cultures, and disciplines.
  • They partner with diversity experts to review datasets and identify gaps.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Use balanced datasets – Ensure your recommendation algorithms include books, articles, and media from underrepresented authors, genres, and regions. ✅ Incorporate demographic data – If possible, adjust algorithms to account for user demographics without reinforcing stereotypes. ✅ Collaborate with faculty and students – Invite diverse stakeholders to review algorithmic outputs for fairness.

Real-World Example: The Harvard Library recently updated its HOLLIS+ search system to include more global perspectives by expanding its metadata tags to reflect non-Western scholarly traditions. They found that after adjustments, search results for African and Indigenous studies increased by 32% within six months.

2. Implement Fairness Metrics in Algorithm Design

Problem: Many libraries use algorithms without measuring whether they produce fair outcomes for all users.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library employs fairness-aware machine learning techniques, tracking metrics like:
    • Disparate impact (do certain groups get fewer recommendations?)
    • Representation parity (are all disciplines equally visible?)
    • Explainability (can users understand why they received certain suggestions?)

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Define fairness KPIs – Set clear metrics for algorithmic equity (e.g., "At least 20% of top recommendations must come from underrepresented authors"). ✅ Use fairness libraries – Tools like Aequitas (for Python) or Fairlearn can help detect bias in recommendation systems. ✅ Conduct A/B testing – Compare biased vs. unbiased algorithms to measure real-world impact.

Real-World Example: The New York Public Library (NYPL) used fairness metrics when redesigning its OverDrive e-book platform. By adjusting their algorithm to prioritize local authors and diverse genres, they saw a 25% increase in checkouts from minority communities within a year.

3. Prioritize Open Access and Alternative Publishers

Problem: Traditional academic publishers often dominate search results, creating a paywall barrier for students from lower-income backgrounds.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Auraria Library actively promotes open-access repositories (like CORE and Unpaywall) in its search algorithms.
  • They weight open-access journals higher in discovery tools like WorldCat Discovery.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Integrate open-access databases – Use APIs from DOAJ (Directory of Open Access Journals) or Unpaywall to boost fair access. ✅ Advocate for institutional repositories – Encourage faculty to deposit research in institutional repositories (e.g., CU Scholar at Auraria’s partner institutions). ✅ Offer alternative discovery tools – Consider Zotero or Mendeley for users who struggle with traditional search engines.

Real-World Example: The University of Michigan Library implemented an open-access bias adjustment in its Carlson Library search system. By boosting open-access results by 30%, they reduced paywall-related frustration by 40% in student surveys.

4. Train Staff on Algorithmic Fairness

Problem: Many librarians lack technical training on how algorithms work, leading to unintentional bias in system design.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library offers workshops on algorithmic fairness, covering:
    • How bias propagates in machine learning.
    • Ethical considerations in AI-driven library systems.
    • Tools for auditing bias.
  • They also hire data ethicists to consult on new projects.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Host training sessions – Partner with local universities or ALA’s Office for Diversity, Literacy, and Outreach for workshops. ✅ Create internal guidelines – Develop a library-specific AI ethics policy outlining fairness standards. ✅ Encourage interdisciplinary collaboration – Work with computer science departments to integrate fairness into algorithm design.

Real-World Example: The University of California, Berkeley Library launched an Algorithmic Fairness Certificate Program for staff. After implementation, librarians reported a 50% increase in awareness of bias in discovery tools, leading to better-informed system upgrades.

5. Use Explainable AI (XAI) for Transparency

Problem: Users often don’t understand why they’re getting certain recommendations, leading to distrust in automated systems.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library provides explainable AI features in its Auraria Library Search interface, showing users:
    • Why a book was recommended (e.g., "Based on your searches for climate justice").
    • How to override recommendations if they feel they’re biased.
  • They also publish algorithmic transparency reports annually.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Add "Why This Recommendation?" – Include a brief explanation in your discovery tool. ✅ Offer manual override options – Let users bypass algorithmic suggestions if they prefer. ✅ Publish fairness reports – Share annual transparency documents detailing algorithmic adjustments.

Real-World Example: The Stanford University Libraries introduced "Explainable Search" in their SearchWorks system. Users could now see how their queries were weighted and why certain results appeared first. This led to a 30% increase in user satisfaction in feedback surveys.

6. Engage Users in Co-Designing Algorithmic Systems

Problem: Algorithms are often designed without user input, leading to systems that don’t meet real needs.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library conducts user testing sessions with diverse student groups, including:
    • First-generation college students.
    • Students with disabilities.
    • Non-traditional learners.
  • They use feedback to refine algorithms, ensuring they reflect user preferences rather than just historical data.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Conduct focus groups – Invite marginalized student populations to test algorithms. ✅ Use iterative design – Continuously update algorithms based on user feedback. ✅ Create a "Bias Reporting" System – Allow users to flag unfair recommendations for review.

Real-World Example: The University of Washington Libraries implemented a "Library Algorithm Advisory Board" consisting of students, faculty, and librarians. After six months, their discovery tool’s recommendations improved fairness scores by 28% based on user input.

7. Address Bias in Metadata and Classification Systems

Problem: Many library catalogs use outdated classification systems (like Library of Congress Subject Headings) that may reinforce stereotypes.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library audits and updates metadata to:
    • Replace problematic terms (e.g., "Native American" → "Indigenous Peoples").
    • Expand subject tags to include global perspectives.
  • They also train catalogers on inclusive metadata practices.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Review and revise subject headings – Use LCSH updates or Library of Congress’s new guidelines. ✅ Train staff on inclusive language – Offer workshops on bias-free cataloging. ✅ Leverage community input – Work with local cultural organizations to suggest better tags.

Real-World Example: The New York Public Library reclassified thousands of records after a 2022 audit revealed racially biased subject headings. By updating terms like "African-American Studies" to "Black Studies", they improved discovery for marginalized authors by 22%.

8. Develop Bias Mitigation Policies and Governance

Problem: Without formal policies, libraries may unintentionally perpetuate bias in their systems.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library has established an Algorithmic Fairness Task Force, which:
    • Reviews all new AI integrations for bias risks.
    • Sets fairness benchmarks for discovery tools.
    • Monitors algorithmic performance regularly.
  • They also include fairness clauses in vendor contracts for discovery software.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Create an AI Ethics Committee – Assign a dedicated group to oversee algorithmic fairness. ✅ Include fairness in RFPs – When purchasing new software, require bias audits. ✅ Develop a bias response plan – Outline steps to take if bias is detected.

Real-World Example: The University of California System implemented a cross-campus AI Governance Policy in 2023, requiring all libraries to conduct annual bias audits of their discovery tools. This led to system-wide improvements in equitable access.

9. Educate Students on Algorithmic Literacy

Problem: Even if libraries fix their algorithms, users may not recognize bias in recommendations.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library offers workshops on algorithmic literacy, teaching students:
    • How recommendation systems work.
    • How to evaluate fairness in search results.
    • Alternative search strategies (e.g., using Boolean operators to bypass algorithmic biases).
  • They also integrate bias awareness into information literacy courses.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Host bias-awareness sessions – Partner with computer science departments for guest lectures. ✅ Create self-paced guides – Develop online modules on recognizing algorithmic bias. ✅ Encourage critical thinking – Teach students to cross-check recommendations with multiple sources.

Real-World Example: The MIT Libraries launched an "Algorithmic Literacy Initiative", where students learned to audit search results for bias. After the program, 85% of participants reported being more skeptical of automated recommendations.

10. Advocate for Policy Changes at Higher Levels

Problem: Some biases are systemic, rooted in university policies or publisher practices.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • The library collaborates with university leadership to:
    • Push for open-access mandates.
    • Advocate for diverse publisher representation in institutional subscriptions.
    • Lobby for algorithmic fairness in higher education tech standards.
  • They also participate in industry consortia (like Portico and COUNTER) to influence fairer discovery metrics.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This: ✅ Lobby for institutional changes – Work with university administration to adopt fairer tech policies. ✅ Join advocacy groups – Engage with ALA’s Office for Information Technology Policy (OITP). ✅ Push for industry standards – Advocate for fairness benchmarks in library tech vendors.

Real-World Example: The Association of Research Libraries (ARL) successfully lobbied for fairer usage statistics in COUNTER standards, ensuring that open-access and alternative publishers are counted equally. This has reduced paywall disparities in many academic libraries.

Common Mistakes in Addressing Algorithmic Bias (And How to Avoid Them)

While Auraria Library’s approach is commendable, many institutions make critical errors when trying to reduce bias. Here’s what to avoid:

❌ Mistake 1: Assuming "Fixing the Algorithm" is Enough

Why it’s wrong: Algorithms are only as fair as the data and policies behind them. Simply tweaking an algorithm without systemic changes won’t eliminate bias.

How to avoid it: ✔ Combine technical fixes with policy changes (e.g., update metadata and train staff). ✔ Monitor long-term impact – Bias can re-emerge if not continuously audited.

❌ Mistake 2: Relying Only on Historical Data

Why it’s wrong: If your dataset is already biased, the algorithm will reproduce those biases.

How to avoid it: ✔ Use synthetic data to supplement real-world data. ✔ Incorporate real-time feedback from users.

❌ Mistake 3: Ignoring User Diversity in Testing

Why it’s wrong: If your test group is homogeneous, you won’t catch group-specific biases.

How to avoid it: ✔ Conduct inclusive user testing with diverse populations. ✔ Partner with community organizations to identify gaps.
