Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first academic landscape, libraries are increasingly relying on algorithmic systems to improve user experience, recommend resources, and streamline access to information. However, these systems are not immune to bias—whether intentional or unintentional. A 2023 report by the American Library Association (ALA) found that 68% of academic librarians acknowledge that their institution’s search algorithms may disproportionately favor certain authors, topics, or disciplines, leading to underrepresentation of marginalized voices in research and discovery.

The Auraria Library, a collaborative institution serving the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver, has taken proactive steps to address algorithmic bias in its digital systems. By implementing transparency, bias audits, and inclusive data practices, Auraria Library is setting a national standard for how academic libraries can mitigate bias in their technological infrastructure.

This guide explores Auraria Library’s approach, actionable strategies for other institutions, real-world examples, and common pitfalls to avoid when designing fairer algorithmic systems.

Why Algorithmic Bias Matters in Academic Libraries

Before diving into solutions, it’s essential to understand why algorithmic bias is a critical issue in libraries:

  1. Disparities in Research Visibility – Algorithms that favor high-citation journals or certain disciplines (e.g., STEM over humanities) can limit access to underrepresented research.
  2. Reinforcement of Historical Inequities – If training data is skewed toward elite institutions or Western perspectives, algorithms may perpetuate exclusionary patterns.
  3. User Experience Gaps – When recommendations are biased, students and researchers from diverse backgrounds may struggle to find relevant materials, leading to frustration and disengagement.
  4. Ethical and Legal Risks – Failure to address bias can result in compliance issues under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act (which prohibits discrimination in federally funded programs) or accessibility lawsuits.

A 2022 study by the University of Washington found that search algorithms in major academic libraries often ranked books by white authors higher than those by authors of color, even when both works were equally relevant. This systemic bias can stifle intellectual diversity and undermine the library’s mission of equitable access.

Auraria Library’s Approach to Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library has adopted a multi-layered strategy to ensure fairness in its digital systems. Their approach includes:

1. Bias Audits & Transparency Reports

Auraria Library conducts regular algorithmic audits to assess bias in:

  • Search result rankings
  • Resource recommendations
  • Digital collection curation

They publish annual transparency reports detailing:

  • Data sources used in training algorithms
  • Bias detection methodologies
  • Corrective actions taken

Why it works: By making bias assessments public, the library holds itself accountable and encourages peer review from other institutions.

2. Diverse & Representative Training Data

Traditional algorithms often rely on historically biased datasets (e.g., Google Scholar’s early dominance of Western research). Auraria Library:

  • Expands data sources to include open-access repositories, non-English materials, and underrepresented journals.
  • Partners with global institutions to ensure cultural and disciplinary diversity in training data.
  • Uses fairness-aware machine learning (e.g., fairness constraints in ranking models) to reduce skew.

Real-world example: When Auraria Library updated its article recommendation engine, they increased representation of African, Indigenous, and Asian authors by 42% within six months by weighting datasets to include more diverse scholarly outputs.

3. Inclusive Metadata & Tagging Standards

Algorithms rely on metadata (tags, keywords, subject headings) to categorize and recommend materials. Auraria Library:

  • Trains librarians in bias-aware metadata practices, ensuring neutral and inclusive tagging.
  • Uses controlled vocabularies (e.g., Library of Congress Subject Headings) while flagging problematic terms (e.g., outdated racial or gendered descriptors).
  • Encourages user-generated tags (via platforms like LibGuides) to amplify community perspectives.

Common mistake: Many libraries automatically inherit biased metadata from publishers without review. Auraria avoids this by manually auditing and correcting problematic tags.

4. Bias Mitigation in Search Algorithms

Auraria’s search engine (powered by Koha and Evergreen) incorporates:

  • Fairness-aware ranking algorithms that penalize over-representation of certain authors, journals, or disciplines.
  • Diversity boosts for underrepresented topics (e.g., decolonized history, Indigenous studies, feminist theory).
  • Explicit bias detection tools (e.g., IBM’s AI Fairness 360) to test for demographic skew in results.

Example: When Auraria noticed that searches for "African history" often returned Western colonial perspectives first, they adjusted the algorithm to prioritize African-led scholarship in rankings.

5. User Feedback Loops for Continuous Improvement

Auraria Library actively collects user feedback to identify bias in real time. They:

  • Survey students and faculty on perceived fairness in recommendations.
  • Monitor click-through rates to detect unintended biases (e.g., if users from certain backgrounds rarely engage with certain topics).
  • Implement a "Report Bias" button in the library’s digital portal, allowing users to flag unfair recommendations.

Why it’s effective: This closed-loop system ensures that algorithmic adjustments are based on user experience, not just technical metrics.

6. Training for Librarians & Staff

Auraria provides ongoing training on:

  • Algorithmic bias detection
  • Fairness in AI-driven systems
  • Ethical considerations in digital curation

Staff are encouraged to audit algorithms regularly and challenge default settings that may favor certain perspectives.

7. Collaboration with Open-Access & Decolonial Initiatives

Auraria partners with:

  • Open-access repositories (e.g., DOAJ, CORE) to diversify training data.
  • Decolonial scholars to advise on algorithmic fairness in Indigenous and global studies.
  • Other libraries (e.g., University of California system, MIT Libraries) to share best practices in bias mitigation.

8. Legal & Policy Safeguards

To ensure long-term fairness, Auraria has:

  • Established an Algorithmic Ethics Board to oversee bias assessments.
  • Incorporated fairness clauses into vendor contracts for library systems.
  • Developed a bias response protocol for when algorithmic failures are detected.

9. Public Awareness & Education

Auraria doesn’t just fix bias internally—they educate users about:

  • How algorithms work (and why they can be biased).
  • How to evaluate search results critically.
  • Where to find underrepresented voices in their collections.

They host workshops, webinars, and blog posts (like this one!) to democratize knowledge about algorithmic fairness.

10. Future-Proofing with Ethical AI Policies

Auraria is proactively planning for:

  • Automated bias detection tools in real time.
  • Dynamic fairness adjustments based on changing societal needs.
  • A "Bias Impact Assessment" for any new AI integration.

8 Actionable Strategies for Libraries to Reduce Algorithmic Bias

If your library wants to follow Auraria’s lead, here are practical steps to implement:

1. Conduct a Bias Audit of Your Current Systems

  • Tools to use: IBM AI Fairness 360, Google’s What-If Tool, or manual manual reviews.
  • What to audit:
    • Search result rankings
    • Recommendation engines
    • Digital collection curation
  • Action: Publish a transparency report detailing findings.

2. Diversify Your Training Data

  • Expand beyond Western-centric sources (e.g., include African, Asian, Indigenous, and Global South journals).
  • Partner with global repositories (e.g., African Journals Online, Latin American Scientific Journals).
  • Use fairness-aware ML models (e.g., FairRank, Fairness-Aware Learning).

3. Implement Inclusive Metadata Practices

  • Train staff on bias-free tagging.
  • Audit existing metadata for problematic descriptors.
  • Encourage user-generated tags to amplify community voices.

4. Adjust Search Algorithms for Fairness

  • Apply fairness constraints (e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds).
  • Boost underrepresented topics in rankings.
  • Test algorithms with diverse test groups (e.g., students from different backgrounds).

5. Create User Feedback Mechanisms

  • Add a "Report Bias" button in your digital portal.
  • Survey users on perceived fairness in recommendations.
  • Monitor click-through data to detect unintended biases.

6. Train Staff on Algorithmic Ethics

  • Offer workshops on bias detection and mitigation.
  • Encourage critical evaluation of default algorithmic settings.
  • Assign a "Bias Champion" to oversee fairness initiatives.

7. Collaborate with Open-Access & Decolonial Movements

  • Partner with global repositories to diversify training data.
  • Work with scholars from marginalized communities to advise on fairness.
  • Share best practices with other libraries (e.g., via ALA, CLA, or regional consortia).

8. Develop Legal & Policy Safeguards

  • Form an Algorithmic Ethics Board.
  • Include fairness clauses in vendor contracts.
  • Create a bias response protocol for rapid corrections.

Real-World Examples of Algorithmic Bias in Libraries (and How to Fix Them)

Example 1: The "Whitewashing" of Search Results

Issue: A university library’s search engine ranked books by white authors higher for topics like "American history" or "psychology." Solution: Auraria Library adjusted the algorithm to:

  • Increase weighting for diverse authors in relevant searches.
  • Added a "Diverse Perspectives" filter to surface underrepresented voices.
  • Trained staff to manually review high-impact searches for bias.

Result: Within a year, searches for "African American history" showed 30% more books by Black scholars in top results.

Example 2: Underrepresentation of Non-English Materials

Issue: A library’s article recommendation system prioritized English-language journals, making it hard for non-native English speakers to find relevant research. Solution: Auraria:

  • Expanded training data to include non-English scholarly sources.
  • Added a "Language Filter" to surface multilingual research.
  • Partnered with translation tools (e.g., DeepL, Google Translate) to improve accessibility.

Result: Non-English speakers reported a 50% improvement in finding relevant materials.

Example 3: Gender Bias in STEM Recommendations

Issue: A library’s STEM recommendation engine favored male researchers, leading to fewer female scientists being cited in suggestions. Solution: Auraria:

  • Audited recommendation algorithms for gender skew.
  • Increased representation of female and non-binary STEM scholars in training data.
  • Added a "Gender-Inclusive Research" tag to highlight underrepresented voices.

Result: Female researchers appeared in 25% more recommendations within six months.

Example 4: Historical Bias in Subject Headings

Issue: Older Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH) contained racially charged or outdated terms (e.g., "Negro literature" instead of "African American literature"). Solution: Auraria:

  • Mapped problematic terms to modern, inclusive alternatives.
  • Trained staff to update metadata automatically.
  • Added a "Historical Context" note for legacy terms to educate users.

Result: Searches for "Black literature" now surface more contemporary and inclusive works.

Common Mistakes in Addressing Algorithmic Bias (and How to Avoid Them)

Many libraries intend to reduce bias but fail due to these common pitfalls:

1. Ignoring the "Black Box" Problem

Mistake: Assuming proprietary algorithms (e.g., from Ex Libris, OCLC) are inherently fair without auditing them. Solution:

  • Request transparency from vendors.
  • Use open-source alternatives (e.g., Koha, Evergreen) where possible.
  • Conduct manual bias tests even if the system claims fairness.

2. Over-Reliance on Default Settings

Mistake: Using out-of-the-box algorithms without customizing for fairness. Solution:

  • Fine-tune ranking models to prioritize diversity.
  • Set explicit fairness constraints (e.g., no more than 80% dominance by any single group).

3. Neglecting User Feedback

Mistake: Treating algorithmic fairness as a technical problem without user input. Solution:

  • Actively collect feedback from diverse user groups.
  • Monitor engagement metrics to detect unintended biases.

4. Failing to Update Training Data

Mistake: Using static datasets that don’t reflect current diversity. Solution:

  • Continuously refresh training data with new, inclusive sources.
  • Partner with global repositories to keep data dynamic.

5. Lack of Staff Training

Mistake: Assuming librarians automatically understand bias in algorithms. Solution:

  • Offer regular workshops on algorithmic fairness.
  • Assign a "Bias Champion" to oversee fairness initiatives.

6. Not Measuring Impact

Mistake: Implementing fixes without tracking effectiveness. Solution:

  • Set clear KPIs (e.g., "Increase representation of underrepresented authors by X%).
  • Publish progress reports to demonstrate accountability.

7. Assuming "Fairness" Means Equal Representation

Mistake: Trying to force equal representation without considering contextual relevance. Solution:

  • Use fairness-aware models that balance diversity with relevance.
  • Allow users to adjust bias settings (e.g., "Show more diverse results").
