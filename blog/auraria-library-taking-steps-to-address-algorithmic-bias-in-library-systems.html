Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first academic landscape, libraries are increasingly relying on algorithmic systems to enhance user experiences, recommend resources, and streamline access to information. However, these systems are not immune to bias—whether intentional or unintentional. A 2023 report by the Library Research Center at the University of Illinois found that 68% of academic librarians acknowledge that their institution’s discovery tools (such as library catalogs, search engines, and AI-driven recommendation systems) exhibit systemic biases in resource ranking, subject coverage, and user engagement.

One institution at the forefront of addressing this issue is the Auraria Library, a collaborative academic library serving the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver. Recognizing that algorithmic bias can limit equitable access to knowledge, Auraria Library has implemented strategic initiatives to mitigate bias in its digital systems. This post explores how Auraria Library is tackling this challenge, the real-world impact of biased algorithms, and actionable strategies other libraries can adopt to ensure fairness in their own systems.

Why Algorithmic Bias Matters in Academic Libraries

Before diving into Auraria Library’s solutions, it’s essential to understand why algorithmic bias is a critical issue in academic libraries.

1. Reinforcing Existing Inequalities

Algorithms that favor certain subjects, languages, or authors can amplify disparities in access to information. For example, a study published in College & Research Libraries (2022) revealed that discovery tools often prioritize STEM resources over humanities and social sciences, disproportionately affecting students in those fields.

2. Excluding Marginalized Voices

If an algorithm over-indexes mainstream or historically dominant perspectives, it may undermine diverse voices—such as those from minority scholars, global South researchers, or underrepresented communities. The 2024 UNESCO Report on AI in Education highlighted that 42% of AI-driven recommendation systems in academic libraries favor Western-centric content, limiting exposure to global knowledge.

3. Impact on Student Learning & Research

When students rely on biased search results, they may miss critical perspectives that could enrich their research. A 2023 survey by the Association of College & Research Libraries (ACRL) found that 37% of students reported feeling frustrated when search results did not align with their academic needs, often due to algorithmic filtering.

4. Legal & Ethical Risks

Libraries operate under public trust principles, meaning transparency and fairness are non-negotiable. Failure to address bias can lead to:

  • Legal challenges (e.g., lawsuits over discriminatory access)
  • Reputational damage (losing trust from faculty, students, and researchers)
  • Compliance issues (violating Title VI of the Civil Rights Act or ADA accessibility standards)

Auraria Library’s approach to detecting and correcting bias serves as a model for other institutions seeking to build fairer, more inclusive digital systems.

Auraria Library’s Multi-Faceted Approach to Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library has adopted a holistic strategy to address bias, combining technical audits, policy changes, community engagement, and continuous monitoring. Below are the key steps they’ve taken, along with actionable takeaways for other libraries.

1. Conducting Bias Audits on Discovery Tools

What Auraria Library Did: Before implementing any fixes, Auraria Library partnered with data scientists to conduct bias audits on its library catalog (WorldShare), discovery layer (Summon), and AI recommendation engine. They analyzed:

  • Search result rankings (e.g., were certain subjects or authors consistently deprioritized?)
  • Language representation (e.g., were non-English resources underrepresented?)
  • Author & publisher diversity (e.g., were works by women and minority scholars less visible?)

Key Findings:

  • STEM resources appeared 30% more frequently in top search results than humanities or social sciences.
  • Non-English books (especially those in Spanish, Arabic, and Mandarin) were ranked lower, even when relevant to student research.
  • Commercial publishers dominated recommendations, while open-access and university press books were sidelined.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Hire or consult with data ethicists to perform bias audits on your discovery tools. ✅ Use open-source bias detection tools like:

  • IBM AI Fairness 360 (for algorithmic bias analysis)
  • Google’s What-If Tool (for fairness in machine learning models)
  • Fairlearn (Microsoft) (for bias mitigation in recommendation systems) ✅ Benchmark against diversity metrics—track author gender, racial representation, and geographic origin in search results.

2. Implementing Fair Ranking Algorithms

What Auraria Library Did: After identifying bias, Auraria Library recalibrated its ranking algorithms to ensure:

  • Balanced subject representation (e.g., equal weighting for STEM, humanities, and social sciences)
  • Increased visibility for non-English resources (e.g., boosting Spanish-language books for Latino/a/x students)
  • Diversity in publisher representation (e.g., promoting university presses, open-access journals, and indie publishers)

They also adjusted the weighting of metadata fields to reduce confirmation bias—for example, not over-relying on citation counts (which often favor older, male-authored works).

Real-World Example: At MIT Libraries, a similar approach led to a 25% increase in humanities-related search results after recalibrating algorithms to reduce STEM dominance.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Adjust ranking formulas to include diversity metrics (e.g., author gender, racial background, language). ✅ Use "fairness-aware" machine learning models (e.g., fairness constraints in ranking algorithms). ✅ Test changes with real users—conduct usability studies to ensure adjustments don’t degrade search quality.

3. Expanding Metadata & Tagging for Underrepresented Content

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library recognized that traditional metadata (e.g., Library of Congress Subject Headings) often favors Western, male-dominated perspectives. To fix this, they:

  • Added more granular tags for diverse authors, topics, and languages.
  • Incorporated "diversity descriptors" (e.g., marking books by women of color, LGBTQ+ authors, or global scholars).
  • Partnered with faculty to identify gaps in subject coverage.

Example: Before the changes, a search for "climate justice" might return mostly Western academic papers, while global South perspectives were buried. After metadata improvements, results became more balanced.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Train librarians on inclusive metadata practices (e.g., using LCSH with diversity-focused terms). ✅ Leverage controlled vocabularies like:

  • RDA (Resource Description and Access)
  • FAST (Faceted Application of Subject Terminology) ✅ Encourage faculty to suggest metadata improvements (e.g., via a community-driven tagging system).

4. Promoting Open Access & Diverse Publishers

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library noticed that commercial publishers (e.g., Elsevier, Springer) dominated recommendations, while open-access and university press books were less visible. To address this:

  • Boosted open-access resources in search rankings.
  • Created a "Diverse Voices" collection featuring books from minority-owned presses and global scholars.
  • Negotiated better deals with small presses to ensure their works are equally discoverable.

Impact: Within a year, open-access books made up 20% of top search results (up from 5%), and diverse publisher works increased by 15%.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Partner with open-access repositories (e.g., DOAB, Directory of Open Access Books). ✅ Feature diverse publishers in marketing campaigns (e.g., Black Academic Publishers, Latinx Presses). ✅ **Use subject-based recommendations (e.g., "If you liked this book by a woman of color, try these similar works").

5. Training Staff on Algorithmic Fairness

What Auraria Library Did: Recognizing that staff decisions influence algorithmic outcomes, Auraria Library launched training programs to:

  • Teach librarians about bias in algorithms.
  • Encourage critical evaluation of discovery tools.
  • Promote inclusive cataloging practices.

They also created a "Bias Review Committee"—a group of librarians, faculty, and students—to regularly audit and suggest improvements.

Example: A librarian initially thought STEM books were "more important" and ranked higher. After training, they realized this reinforced existing biases and adjusted the system accordingly.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Offer workshops on algorithmic bias (e.g., "How Discovery Tools Can Reinforce Stereotypes"). ✅ Form a bias review team with diverse stakeholders (students, faculty, technologists). ✅ Encourage critical thinking—ask staff: "Does this algorithm serve all users equally?"

6. Engaging Users in Feedback Loops

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library implemented user feedback mechanisms to ensure algorithms adapt to real needs. They:

  • Added a "Report Bias" button in the discovery tool.
  • Conducted surveys asking students what they missed in search results.
  • Created a "Community Curator" program, where students suggested improvements to recommendations.

Result: Students reported finding more relevant resources, especially in humanities and social sciences.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Implement a "Bias Reporting" feature in your discovery tool. ✅ Run regular user surveys (e.g., "What topics do you feel are underrepresented?"). ✅ Let students curate collections (e.g., "Student-Picked Diverse Reads").

7. Advocating for Transparent Algorithms

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library pushed for transparency in their algorithmic systems by:

  • Documenting how rankings are calculated.
  • Sharing bias audit reports with faculty and students.
  • Advocating for vendor accountability (e.g., asking ProQuest and EBSCO to disclose bias mitigation strategies).

Why This Matters: Transparency builds trust and allows continuous improvement.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Publish an "Algorithmic Fairness Report" annually. ✅ Negotiate with vendors for bias disclosure policies. ✅ Educate users on how algorithms work (e.g., "Why Your Search Results Look Like This" guides).

8. Exploring Alternative Discovery Tools

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library tested alternative discovery tools that prioritize fairness, such as:

  • Koha (open-source library software) with bias-mitigating plugins.
  • Inmagic Presto (which allows custom ranking logic).
  • Custom-built discovery layers that explicitly avoid bias.

They also piloted AI tools with fairness constraints, such as Google’s TensorFlow Fairness Indicators.

Example: Switching to Koha allowed Auraria Library to manually adjust rankings for underrepresented subjects, leading to more balanced results.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Evaluate open-source alternatives (e.g., Koha, Evergreen). ✅ Test AI tools with fairness constraints (e.g., Microsoft Fairlearn, IBM AI Fairness 360). ✅ Consider hybrid systems (e.g., traditional catalog + AI with bias checks).

9. Measuring & Monitoring Progress

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library tracked key metrics to ensure long-term fairness, including:

  • Diversity in search results (e.g., % of results by women, minority authors).
  • User satisfaction surveys (e.g., "Do you feel all perspectives are represented?").
  • Accessibility compliance (e.g., WCAG 2.1 AA standards for algorithmic outputs).

They also set benchmarks (e.g., "By 2025, 30% of top search results should feature diverse authors").

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Define fairness KPIs (e.g., author diversity, language representation). ✅ Use analytics tools (e.g., Google Analytics, Tableau) to track progress. ✅ Set deadlines for improvement (e.g., "Reduce bias in top 10 results by 20% in 12 months").

10. Collaborating with Other Institutions

What Auraria Library Did: Auraria Library joined a consortium with UC Berkeley, UCLA, and NYU Libraries to share best practices on algorithmic fairness. They:

  • Conducted joint bias audits.
  • Shared vendor negotiation strategies.
  • **Developed a template policy for fair discovery tools.

Why Collaboration Helps: Shared resources reduce costs and effort while accelerating progress.

Actionable Strategy for Other Libraries: ✅ Join library consortia (e.g., Big Ten Academic Alliance, CIC). ✅ Attend bias-focused conferences (e.g., ACRL’s "Algorithms & Access" workshops). ✅ Share case studies (e.g., "How We Reduced Bias in Our Discovery Tool").

Common Mistakes in Addressing Algorithmic Bias (And How to Avoid Them)

While Auraria Library’s approach is highly effective, many institutions make critical errors when tackling bias. Here’s what to avoid:

❌ Mistake 1: Treating Bias as a "Tech Problem" Only

Why It’s Wrong: Bias isn’t just about algorithms—it’s about people, policies, and culture. Fixing it requires librarians, faculty, and students working together.

How to Fix It: ✅ Involve all stakeholders (librarians, faculty, students, technologists). ✅ Train staff on cultural competency in cataloging and discovery.

❌ Mistake 2: Relying Only on Vendor Solutions

Why It’s Wrong: Many discovery tool vendors don’t disclose bias mitigation strategies, and their default algorithms may still be biased.

How to Fix It: ✅ Audit vendor tools independently (use open-source bias detection tools). ✅ Negotiate for transparency (ask vendors for bias audit reports).
