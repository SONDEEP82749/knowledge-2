Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first academic landscape, libraries are increasingly relying on algorithmic systems to recommend resources, manage collections, and enhance user experiences. However, these systems are not immune to bias—whether intentional or unintentional. A 2023 report by the Library Research Group found that 68% of academic librarians acknowledge that their institutions’ digital systems exhibit some form of bias in resource recommendations, search results, or access policies.

The Auraria Library, a collaborative institution serving the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver, has taken proactive steps to address this issue. By implementing fairness audits, transparent algorithms, and user-centered design, Auraria Library is setting a benchmark for ethical AI integration in academic libraries.

This blog post explores how Auraria Library is tackling algorithmic bias, the strategies they’ve adopted, and actionable lessons for other libraries looking to improve fairness in their systems.

Understanding Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before diving into Auraria Library’s approach, it’s essential to understand what algorithmic bias is and how it manifests in library environments.

What Is Algorithmic Bias?

Algorithmic bias occurs when machine learning models, recommendation engines, or automated systems produce unfair or discriminatory outcomes based on historical data, training biases, or flawed design. In libraries, this can lead to:

  • Over-representation or under-representation of certain topics, authors, or genres.
  • Unequal access to resources based on user demographics (e.g., race, socioeconomic status).
  • Recommendation bias, where certain books, journals, or databases are favored over others without merit.
  • Search result disparities, where queries from different users yield inconsistent or biased results.

How Does This Affect Academic Libraries?

A 2022 study in the Journal of Academic Librarianship revealed that:

  • 42% of students reported feeling frustrated when search results did not align with their research needs.
  • 30% of faculty members noted that algorithmic recommendations often reinforced existing knowledge gaps rather than expanding them.
  • 28% of librarians admitted that their catalog systems had unintentionally excluded certain disciplines (e.g., humanities vs. STEM).

Auraria Library recognized these challenges and decided to actively combat bias through structured interventions.

Auraria Library’s Approach to Combating Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library’s strategy is multi-faceted, combining technical audits, policy changes, and community engagement. Below are the key steps they’ve taken to ensure fairness in their systems.

1. Conducting Fairness Audits of Existing Systems

Problem: Many libraries rely on proprietary search engines and recommendation algorithms (e.g., OCLC’s WorldShare, Ex Libris Alma) without knowing how biased they may be.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Third-party fairness audits – The library partnered with AI ethics researchers to evaluate their catalog search, recommendation engine, and access control systems.
  • Bias detection tools – They used open-source bias detection frameworks (e.g., IBM’s AI Fairness 360) to analyze search result distributions across different user groups.
  • Discipline-specific analysis – They examined whether STEM, humanities, and social sciences were equally represented in recommendations.

Real-World Example: During their audit, Auraria discovered that their journal recommendation system favored high-impact STEM journals over humanities and social sciences, leading to underrepresentation of certain research areas. By adjusting weighting algorithms, they improved disciplinary balance in recommendations.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Schedule regular fairness audits (annually or bi-annually). ✅ Use open-source bias detection tools (e.g., Fairlearn, Aequitas). ✅ Benchmark against discipline-specific usage data to identify gaps.

2. Implementing Transparent Algorithm Design

Problem: Many libraries use black-box algorithms where users (and even librarians) don’t understand how recommendations are generated.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Algorithm explainability – They developed detailed documentation explaining how their recommendation engine works, including ranking factors (e.g., recency, relevance, user engagement).
  • User-facing transparency – They added a "How We Recommend" section in their library portal, explaining why certain books/journals appear first.
  • Open-source alternatives – Where possible, they migrated to open-source solutions (e.g., Koha, Evergreen) that allow greater customization and auditing.

Real-World Example: Auraria’s fiction book recommendation system initially favored best-selling authors, leading to over-representation of white male authors. By adding diversity metrics (e.g., author demographics, genre diversity) into the algorithm, they improved inclusivity in suggestions.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Document algorithmic decision-making in a publicly accessible policy. ✅ Use explainable AI (XAI) tools to break down how recommendations are made. ✅ Consider open-source alternatives if proprietary systems lack transparency.

3. Diversifying Training Data for Better Representation

Problem: Algorithms learn from historical data, which often reflects existing biases (e.g., over-indexing on Western literature, under-representation of global voices).

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Curated training datasets – They manually reviewed and balanced their training data to ensure fair representation of:
    • Authors from marginalized groups
    • Non-English language materials
    • Underrepresented disciplines (e.g., Indigenous studies, queer theory)
  • Collaboration with faculty – Librarians worked with department heads to identify gaps in their digital collections.
  • Dynamic data updates – They regularly refresh training data to reflect emerging research trends.

Real-World Example: Auraria noticed that their history section recommendations heavily favored European and North American perspectives. By adding more global history sources (e.g., African, Asian, Latin American histories) into the training data, they reduced bias in search results.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Audit your training data for demographic and disciplinary gaps. ✅ Partner with faculty to identify underrepresented resources. ✅ Implement a data refresh cycle (quarterly or annually).

4. Adopting Fairness-by-Design Principles

Problem: Many libraries retrofit fairness into existing systems rather than building it in from the start.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Fairness-by-design workshops – They trained developers and librarians on ethical AI principles, including:
    • Bias mitigation techniques
    • Fairness metrics (e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds)
    • Alternative data sources (e.g., using user behavior logs instead of just click-through rates)
  • Bias impact assessments – Before deploying new algorithms, they conduct bias impact assessments to predict potential fairness issues.

Real-World Example: When Auraria upgraded their e-book lending system, they tested multiple fairness metrics to ensure that students from different socioeconomic backgrounds had equal access to digital resources. They found that some algorithms favored wealthier students (who had faster internet), so they adjusted loan limits to balance access.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Train staff on fairness-by-design principles. ✅ Conduct bias impact assessments before system updates. ✅ Use multiple fairness metrics (not just accuracy).

5. Encouraging User Feedback and Continuous Improvement

Problem: Many libraries assume their systems are fair without actively seeking user input.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Feedback mechanisms – They implemented surveys, suggestion boxes, and direct feedback forms to gather user experiences with algorithmic recommendations.
  • Community advisory boards – They formed a Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) committee with students, faculty, and staff to monitor bias in real time.
  • A/B testing – They tested different algorithm versions with diverse user groups to see which performed best in terms of fairness and relevance.

Real-World Example: Auraria’s student feedback revealed that first-generation college students often struggled to find relevant resources because the algorithm prioritized popular books over foundational texts. By adjusting the recommendation weights, they improved accessibility for underrepresented students.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Implement structured feedback channels (surveys, focus groups). ✅ Form a DEI committee to actively monitor bias. ✅ Use A/B testing to compare fairness across user groups.

6. Addressing Bias in Access Control and Interlibrary Loan Systems

Problem: Some libraries unintentionally restrict access based on user location, institution, or demographic factors.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Access fairness reviews – They audited their interlibrary loan (ILL) system to ensure that all students, regardless of institution, had equal borrowing privileges.
  • Fair usage policies – They removed arbitrary limits on e-book loans for low-income students.
  • Geographic access adjustments – They expanded digital access for off-campus users to reduce digital divides.

Real-World Example: Auraria discovered that community college students (who used the library less frequently) were less likely to receive timely ILL requests due to systemic delays. By prioritizing their requests and streamlining approvals, they reduced inequities in resource access.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Review ILL policies for unintended access barriers. ✅ Remove arbitrary usage limits for underrepresented groups. ✅ Expand digital access for off-campus and part-time students.

7. Promoting Algorithmic Literacy Among Staff and Users

Problem: Even if libraries fix algorithmic biases, users may still misinterpret or misuse biased systems.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Workshops on algorithmic fairness – They offered training sessions for librarians, faculty, and students on:
    • How algorithms work
    • How to recognize bias
    • How to advocate for fairer systems
  • Educational resources – They created guides and FAQs explaining how to evaluate algorithmic recommendations.
  • Critical media literacy programs – They integrated AI ethics discussions into information literacy courses.

Real-World Example: Auraria’s faculty training revealed that some professors assumed their journal recommendations were objective, not realizing they favored certain publishers. By educating them on algorithmic biases, they encouraged more diverse reading lists.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Host workshops on algorithmic fairness. ✅ Create educational guides for staff and students. ✅ Integrate AI ethics into information literacy curricula.

8. Advocating for Policy Changes at Institutional and National Levels

Problem: Many libraries operate within biased institutional frameworks (e.g., funding disparities, publisher biases, legacy collection gaps).

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Collaborating with university leadership – They advocated for (and secured) additional funding for diverse digital collections.
  • Lobbying for fairer publisher contracts – They negotiated with vendors to reduce bias in journal rankings.
  • Participating in national initiatives – They joined **groups like the Library Research Group and ALA’s Office for Diversity, Literacy, and Outreach to push for systemic change.

Real-World Example: Auraria partnered with the university to **expand their global studies collection, ensuring that non-Western perspectives were equally represented in recommendations.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Advocate for institutional funding for diverse collections. ✅ Negotiate fairer contracts with publishers and vendors. ✅ Join national initiatives to push for systemic fairness.

9. Using Alternative Metrics Beyond Traditional "Success" Indicators

Problem: Many libraries measure success based on click-through rates, loan counts, or popularity, which reinforce bias.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Fairness metrics – They tracked not just usage but also:
    • Disciplinary diversity in recommendations
    • Author/genre diversity
    • Access equity across user groups
  • Impact assessments – They measured how algorithms affected student research outcomes and faculty publishing trends.
  • Longitudinal studies – They tracked changes over time to see if bias reduction efforts were working.

Real-World Example: Auraria replaced "popularity-based" recommendations with a balanced metric that prioritized relevance, diversity, and accessibility. This led to higher engagement from underrepresented students while maintaining academic rigor.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Move beyond click metrics – Track fairness and diversity. ✅ Assess real-world impact on research and learning. ✅ Conduct long-term studies to measure progress.

10. Building a Culture of Ethical AI in the Library

Problem: Without cultural buy-in, even the best technical fixes may fail over time.

Auraria’s Solution:

  • Ethics committees – They established a standing committee to oversee AI ethics.
  • Incentivizing fairness – They recognized staff who identified and mitigated bias.
  • Open dialogue – They encouraged transparent discussions about algorithmic risks.

Real-World Example: Auraria’s culture shift led to more librarians questioning why certain books were recommended over others, leading to more inclusive collections.

Actionable Takeaway for Other Libraries: ✅ Form an ethics committee to oversee AI decisions. ✅ Reward staff who advocate for fairness. ✅ Foster open discussions about algorithmic risks.

Common Mistakes in Addressing Algorithmic Bias (And How to Avoid Them)

1. Assuming "Fixing the Algorithm" Is Enough

Mistake: Some libraries only tweak algorithms without addressing systemic issues (e.g., underfunded collections, biased training data).
