Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In an era where technology drives nearly every aspect of our lives—from social media feeds to search engines—libraries are not exempt from the influence of algorithms. The Auraria Library, a collaborative institution serving the University of Colorado Denver, Colorado State University Pueblo, and Metropolitan State University of Denver, has recognized the critical need to address algorithmic bias in its digital systems. As libraries increasingly rely on automated tools for resource discovery, recommendation engines, and access control, the risk of reinforcing systemic inequalities becomes a pressing concern.

Recent studies highlight the severity of this issue:

  • A 2023 Pew Research Center report found that 68% of Americans believe search engines and social media platforms often show biased results, with 42% admitting they have encountered discriminatory outcomes in automated systems.
  • The 2022 American Library Association (ALA) Technology in Libraries Report noted that 73% of academic librarians expressed concerns about algorithmic bias affecting user access to information, particularly in discovery tools and digital collections.
  • Research published in the Journal of Documentation (2024) revealed that recommendation algorithms in library systems disproportionately favor popular or commercially successful titles, marginalizing diverse voices, niche research, and underrepresented authors.

Auraria Library, like many modern academic institutions, is taking proactive steps to mitigate these biases. By implementing fairer algorithms, transparent data practices, and inclusive design principles, the library aims to ensure that all users—regardless of background, research focus, or socioeconomic status—have equitable access to knowledge.

This blog post explores how Auraria Library is addressing algorithmic bias, the key strategies they employ, real-world examples of bias in library systems, common mistakes to avoid, and actionable steps other institutions can take to foster fairness in their own digital ecosystems.

Understanding Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before diving into Auraria Library’s initiatives, it’s essential to understand what algorithmic bias is and how it manifests in academic and public libraries.

What Is Algorithmic Bias?

Algorithmic bias occurs when automated systems—such as search engines, recommendation algorithms, or access control tools—produce outcomes that unfairly disadvantage certain groups based on factors like:

  • Race or ethnicity
  • Gender
  • Socioeconomic status
  • Geographical location
  • Disability status
  • Research topic or academic discipline

Unlike human bias, which can be consciously corrected, algorithmic bias often stems from flawed data, biased training sets, or unintended design choices, making it harder to detect and eliminate.

How Does Algorithmic Bias Affect Libraries?

Libraries rely on algorithms in several critical areas:

  1. Resource Discovery & Search Results

    • If a search algorithm favors commercially popular books over scholarly or niche research, users may miss valuable academic sources.
    • Example: A student searching for "climate justice in Indigenous communities" might be overshadowed by results on mainstream environmentalism if the algorithm prioritizes high-circulation titles.

  2. Recommendation Systems

    • Library recommendation engines (similar to Netflix or Amazon) often reinforce echo chambers, suggesting books or articles that align with preexisting biases rather than introducing diverse perspectives.
    • Example: A researcher studying Afrofuturism might receive fewer recommendations on related works if the algorithm overweights Western sci-fi in its training data.

  3. Access Control & Digital Lending

    • Some libraries use automated hold systems that may disproportionately penalize users based on past borrowing patterns, leading to digital redlining—where certain neighborhoods or user groups face restricted access.

  4. Metadata & Classification Systems

    • Traditional Library of Congress Classification (LCC) and Dewey Decimal System have been criticized for reinforcing colonial and Eurocentric biases in how knowledge is organized.
    • Example: Indigenous knowledge systems are often misclassified or excluded from mainstream library catalogs, making them harder to discover.

  5. AI-Powered Chatbots & Virtual Assistants

    • Some libraries use AI chatbots to answer reference questions, but if trained on biased datasets, they may provide inaccurate or exclusionary information.
    • Example: A chatbot might misattribute historical events to a single cultural perspective, reinforcing monocultural narratives.

Why Is This a Problem for Auraria Library?

Auraria Library serves a diverse student body, including:

  • First-generation college students
  • Students from underrepresented racial and ethnic groups
  • Non-traditional learners (working adults, veterans, international students)
  • Researchers in emerging fields (e.g., decolonial studies, critical race theory, disability studies)

If the library’s digital systems perpetuate bias, these groups may face: ✔ Limited access to relevant research ✔ Exclusion from academic discourse ✔ Reduced confidence in the library’s ability to serve them equitably

Auraria Library’s response? A multi-faceted approach to auditing, redesigning, and advocating for fairer algorithms.

Auraria Library’s Strategies to Combat Algorithmic Bias

Auraria Library has implemented several innovative strategies to ensure their digital systems are fair, transparent, and inclusive. Below, we break down eight key actions they are taking, along with real-world examples and lessons for other libraries.

1. Conducting Regular Bias Audits of Search & Recommendation Algorithms

Problem: Many library search engines (like Koha, Alma, or Evergreen) use proprietary algorithms that are black boxes—their inner workings are unknown, making bias detection difficult.

Auraria Library’s Solution:

  • Partnered with data scientists to audit their discovery tool (currently using Alma + Primo) for demographic disparities in search results.
  • Tested queries related to diverse academic fields (e.g., "Latinx literature," "disability rights," "postcolonial feminism") and compared results with neutral or majority-group queries.
  • Found that 32% of search results for underrepresented topics were dominated by commercial publishers, while academic and open-access sources were underserved.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Use open-source tools like Fairlearn (Microsoft) or Aequitas (Microsoft Research) to detect bias in recommendation systems.
  • Benchmark against neutral queries—if a search for "women in STEM" returns fewer results than "men in STEM", that’s a red flag.
  • Document findings and share them publicly (e.g., in an annual transparency report).

Real-World Example: The University of Michigan Library conducted a similar audit and found that their article recommendation system overweighted journals from elite publishers, leading to reduced visibility for community colleges and regional research. They reweighted their algorithm to include more open-access and institutional repositories.

2. Diversifying Training Data for AI & Machine Learning Models

Problem: Many library algorithms are trained on historically biased datasets, such as:

  • Commercial book databases (e.g., OverDrive, Hoopla) that favor bestsellers over niche or academic works.
  • Traditional bibliographic records that undervalue Indigenous, queer, or global South perspectives.

Auraria Library’s Solution:

  • Collaborated with faculty in Ethnic Studies, Women’s & Gender Studies, and Indigenous Knowledge to curate a diverse training dataset.
  • Included books by underrepresented authors in recommendation algorithms, ensuring that students researching marginalized topics see relevant suggestions.
  • **Partnered with Open Library and HathiTrust to supplement commercial data with open-access and community-driven collections.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Work with faculty to identify gaps in current recommendation systems.
  • Use open datasets like:
    • Open Library’s metadata
    • UNESCO’s Global Education Database
    • Indigenous Knowledge Systems repositories
  • Implement "fairness constraints" in machine learning models (e.g., ensuring 30% of recommendations come from diverse publishers).

Real-World Example: The New York Public Library updated its book recommendation engine by **including more works from Pulitzer Prize-winning authors of color and small presses, leading to a 28% increase in diverse book recommendations for users.

3. Implementing Bias-Mitigation Techniques in Search Ranking

Problem: Traditional TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) and PageRank-like algorithms often rank popular items higher, suppressing lesser-known but equally valuable works.

Auraria Library’s Solution:

  • Adopted a hybrid ranking model that balances popularity with diversity.
  • Applied "fairness-aware ranking" (a technique from fair ML research) to penalize over-representation of elite publishers while boosting underrepresented sources.
  • Tested the impact by comparing before-and-after search results for niche topics—results showed a 15% increase in relevant, diverse sources.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Use fairness-aware ranking libraries like:
    • Microsoft’s Fairlearn
    • IBM’s AI Fairness 360
  • Experiment with "diversity-aware retrieval"—this ensures that even if a topic is niche, results are not skewed toward mainstream views.
  • Monitor rankings over time to prevent regression (i.e., ensuring fairness isn’t lost as the system updates).

Real-World Example: The Harvard Library implemented fairness-aware ranking in its article discovery tool and found that students researching "global health disparities" now see more peer-reviewed papers from low-income countries rather than just high-impact journals from the Global North.

4. Enhancing Metadata & Classification for Inclusive Discovery

Problem: Traditional Library of Congress (LC) and Dewey Decimal classifications were designed in an era where Western, male, and Eurocentric perspectives dominated. This leads to:

  • Indigenous knowledge systems being misclassified under "folklore" or "mythology".
  • Queer literature being hidden under "fiction" rather than "LGBTQ+ studies".
  • Disability studies often lumped together with "medical texts" rather than academic discourse.

Auraria Library’s Solution:

  • Worked with faculty to expand metadata tags for diverse topics, including:
    • #IndigenousKnowledge
    • #DecolonialStudies
    • #DisabilityJustice
    • #QueerTheory
  • **Partnered with Library of Congress’s "Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion" team to advocate for better classification of marginalized voices.
  • **Implemented controlled vocabularies that explicitly include non-Western philosophical traditions (e.g., Afrofuturism, Indigenous epistemologies).

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Audit current metadata using tools like Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH) Analyzer.
  • Add custom tags for emerging fields (e.g., "Climate Justice," "Transgender Studies").
  • Train staff on inclusive cataloging practices (e.g., avoiding gendered language in subject headings).

Real-World Example: The University of California, Berkeley Library reclassified thousands of records to better reflect Indigenous knowledge systems, making it easier for students to find **works on Two-Spirit traditions or Māori environmental ethics.

5. Transparency Reports & User Feedback Loops

Problem: Many libraries do not disclose how their algorithms work, making it impossible for users to challenge biased outcomes.

Auraria Library’s Solution:

  • **Published an annual Algorithmic Transparency Report detailing:
    • How search rankings are determined
    • Which datasets are used in recommendations
    • Bias mitigation efforts
  • **Implemented a user feedback system where patrons can flag biased results (e.g., "This search returned no results for Indigenous feminist theory").
  • **Created a Bias Review Board with faculty, students, and librarians to investigate complaints and adjust algorithms accordingly.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Start a transparency initiative—even basic disclosures (e.g., "Our recommendation engine uses 60% commercial data and 40% open-access sources") build trust.
  • **Use tools like Google’s What-If Tool to visualize algorithmic decisions.
  • Encourage user reports via surveys or direct feedback forms.

Real-World Example: The University of Toronto Libraries launched a "Fairness in Discovery" initiative, where users could submit biased search results, leading to **algorithm updates that improved representation of Black Canadian literature by 40%.

6. Decolonizing Digital Collections & Promoting Open Access

Problem: Many library collections still rely heavily on commercial publishers, which favor Western, male, and corporate perspectives.

Auraria Library’s Solution:

  • **Increased funding for open-access journals and Indigenous-led publishing projects.
  • **Partnered with Portico and LOCKSS to preserve digital scholarship from underrepresented voices.
  • **Created a "Decolonizing the Stacks" collection featuring:
    • Indigenous-authored books
    • Postcolonial literature
    • Works by disabled scholars
  • **Advocated for institutional repositories that prioritize local and marginalized research.

How Other Libraries Can Apply This:

  • Shift budgets from commercial databases to open-access alternatives (e.g., DOAB, Directory of Open Access Books).
  • **Support Indigenous and grassroots publishers (e.g., Aboriginal Peoples’ Television Network, Small Press Distribution).
  • **Host workshops on open-access publishing for faculty and students.

Real-World Example: The University of British Columbia Library reduced its reliance on Elsevier by **subscribing to open-access alternatives like PLOS and BioMed Central, leading to greater access to global health research from the Global South.

7. Training Staff on Algorithmic Fairness & Inclusive Design

Problem: Many librarians lack training in how algorithms work or how to audit for bias, leading to unintended reinforcement of systemic inequalities.

Auraria Library’s Solution:

  • Offered workshops on:
    • Bias in machine learning
    • Fairness-aware algorithm design
    • Inclusive metadata practices
  • **Hired a Digital Equity Librarian to oversee algorithmic fairness initiatives.
  • **Created a cross-departmental task force (including IT, cataloging, and public services) to collaborate on bias mitigation.
