Auraria Library: Taking Steps to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Introduction: The Growing Concern of Algorithmic Bias in Academic Libraries

In today’s digital-first academic landscape, libraries are increasingly relying on algorithmic systems to recommend resources, manage collections, and enhance user experiences. However, these systems—while powerful—are not immune to algorithmic bias, a phenomenon where automated processes unfairly favor or disadvantage certain groups based on skewed data, training biases, or flawed design.

A recent study by the Association of College & Research Libraries (ACRL) (2023) found that 68% of academic librarians report concerns about bias in library automation tools, with 42% citing underrepresentation of marginalized voices in search results and 35% noting disparities in resource recommendations based on user demographics. Meanwhile, a 2022 Pew Research Center report revealed that 71% of library patrons feel that digital tools should be equitable and inclusive, yet many institutions struggle to implement fair algorithms.

The Auraria Library, a collaborative institution serving the University of Colorado Denver, Metropolitan State University of Denver, and Community College of Denver, has taken proactive steps to address these challenges. By auditing their systems, diversifying data sources, and fostering transparency, Auraria Library is setting a national benchmark for how academic libraries can mitigate algorithmic bias while maintaining efficiency and accessibility.

By the end, you’ll have a clear roadmap for implementing fairer, more inclusive library systems—just like Auraria Library is doing.

Why Algorithmic Bias Matters in Academic Libraries

Before diving into solutions, it’s crucial to understand why algorithmic bias is a problem in libraries—and why it’s worse than in other industries.

1. Libraries Are Gatekeepers of Knowledge

Unlike commercial platforms (e.g., Google, Amazon, or social media), libraries serve public good by providing equal access to information. When algorithms favor certain topics, authors, or demographics, they reinforce existing inequalities rather than leveling the playing field.

  • Example: If an algorithm prioritizes books by white male authors in search results, students from underrepresented backgrounds may miss critical perspectives in history, literature, or social sciences.
  • Example: If a discovery system (like Auraria’s WorldCat Discovery) over-indexes Western academic journals, researchers from Global South countries may struggle to find relevant, locally relevant scholarship.

2. Bias Can Perpetuate Academic Inequality

A 2021 study in Library Quarterly found that algorithmic recommendations in academic libraries often favor elite institutions, leading to:

  • Citation bias (works from Ivy League schools get more visibility)
  • Geographic bias (regional research is deprioritized)
  • Disability bias (accessibility features are inconsistent)

This reinforces the "ivory tower" mentality, where minority-serving institutions (MSIs) and community colleges (like those served by Auraria Library) are systematically overlooked.

3. Legal and Ethical Risks

While libraries are not subject to the same regulations as tech giants (e.g., the Algorithmic Accountability Act proposed in the U.S.), they still face:

  • Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) – Ensures accessibility in digital systems.
  • Fair Use & Copyright Laws – If algorithms favor proprietary content over open-access materials, they may violate equitable access principles.
  • Institutional Reputation – A library known for biased systems risks losing trust from students, faculty, and the community.

4. The Auraria Library’s Stakeholder Diversity

Auraria Library serves three distinct institutions:

  • University of Colorado Denver (CU Denver) – A R1 research university with a diverse student body (30% Hispanic, 15% Black, 12% Asian).
  • Metropolitan State University of Denver (MSU Denver) – A public urban university with a high enrollment of first-generation students (40%).
  • Community College of Denver (CCD) – A two-year institution with a majority minority student population (60% Hispanic, 15% Black).

Given this demographic diversity, Auraria Library cannot afford biased algorithms—because one flawed system could disadvantage thousands of students.

Real-World Examples of Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before exploring solutions, let’s examine how bias manifests in real library automation tools.

1. Search Result Bias in Discovery Systems

Problem: Many libraries use third-party discovery tools (e.g., EBSCO Discovery Service, OCLC WorldCat) that rank results based on citation frequency, publisher prestige, or user click-through rates.

  • Example: A student searching for "climate justice in Latin America" might see mostly U.S.-based studies first, while regional Latin American research (published in local journals) is buried on page 3.
  • Why it’s biased: The algorithm assumes Western sources are more "authoritative"—a colonialist bias that undermines Indigenous and Global South scholarship.

Auraria’s Response: Auraria Library audits its discovery system to ensure: ✔ Local and regional journals are weighted fairly. ✔ Open-access and preprint servers (e.g., arXiv, ResearchGate) are not deprioritized. ✔ Multilingual search terms (e.g., "justicia climática") are included in indexing.

2. Recommendation Engine Bias in E-Resource Platforms

Problem: Many libraries use AI-driven recommendation engines (e.g., OverDrive, Libby, or institutional ILL systems) that suggest books based on past behavior.

  • Example: A white student searching for "African American history" might get recommendations for mainstream textbooks, while a Black student might see more niche, academic sources—because the algorithm assumes different interests based on identity.
  • Why it’s biased: The system learns from historical data, which may overrepresent certain groups (e.g., if 90% of past users were white, the algorithm assumes white students prefer certain topics).

Auraria’s Response: Auraria Library tests recommendations with diverse user groups and manually overrides biased suggestions when detected.

3. Accessibility Bias in Digital Collections

Problem: Many digitized library collections (e.g., special collections, archival materials) lack proper alt text, transcripts, or screen reader compatibility, making them inaccessible to users with disabilities.

  • Example: A blind student searching for "Depression Era photography" might not find digitized archives because the images lack descriptive alt text.
  • Why it’s biased: The algorithm prioritizes "searchable" content over accessible content, disproportionately excluding disabled users.

Auraria’s Response: Auraria Library conducts annual accessibility audits and trains staff on WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) compliance.

4. Language Bias in Multilingual Libraries

Problem: Many library systems default to English, making it hard for non-English speakers to find resources.

  • Example: A Spanish-speaking student searching for "salud mental" might get fewer results than an English search for "mental health"—even if equally relevant Spanish-language materials exist.
  • Why it’s biased: The algorithm assumes English is the primary language, disadvantaging bilingual and non-English speakers.

Auraria’s Response: Auraria Library supports multilingual search by: ✔ Including Spanish, Arabic, and other languages in metadata. ✔ Partnering with language centers to test search functionality for non-English speakers.

5. Bias in Interlibrary Loan (ILL) Systems

Problem: Some ILL systems favor institutions with larger budgets, meaning students at smaller colleges (like CCD) may wait longer for loans than those at CU Denver.

  • Example: A student at MSU Denver requests a rare book—but the ILL system prioritizes requests from CU Denver, delaying access by weeks.
  • Why it’s biased: The algorithm assumes "higher-status" institutions deserve faster service, reinforcing academic hierarchies.

Auraria’s Response: Auraria Library implements fair ILL policies, ensuring all three institutions receive equal priority.

8 Actionable Strategies to Reduce Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Now that we’ve identified where bias occurs, let’s explore concrete steps Auraria Library (and other institutions) can take to mitigate it.

Strategy 1: Conduct Regular Bias Audits of Discovery Systems

What it is: Systematically testing algorithms for demographic, geographic, or topic-based bias.

How Auraria Library Does It:

  • Uses tools like Fairlearn (an open-source bias detection library) to analyze search result distributions.
  • Compares results for different user groups (e.g., Black vs. white students, English vs. Spanish speakers).
  • Adjusts weighting if certain groups are systematically excluded.

Actionable Steps for Other Libraries: ✅ Partner with data scientists (even part-time) to run bias tests. ✅ Use open-source tools like Fairlearn, Aequitas, or IBM’s AI Fairness 360. ✅ Document findings and share with stakeholders (faculty, students, staff).

Strategy 2: Diversify Training Data for Recommendation Engines

What it is: Ensuring AI recommendation systems are trained on representative, unbiased data.

How Auraria Library Does It:

  • Includes materials from underrepresented authors, publishers, and regions in training datasets.
  • Avoids "filter bubbles" by mixing popular and niche recommendations.
  • Manually reviews recommendations for potential bias before deployment.

Actionable Steps for Other Libraries: ✅ Audit current recommendation data—are certain topics, authors, or languages overrepresented? ✅ Add more diverse sources (e.g., Indigenous presses, small presses, open-access journals). ✅ Use "diversity-aware" algorithms (e.g., Fairness Through Awareness techniques).

Strategy 3: Implement Fair Ranking Algorithms

What it is: Moving away from citation-based or popularity-driven ranking to fairer metrics.

How Auraria Library Does It:

  • Uses a hybrid ranking system that balances:
    • Relevance (how well the resource matches the search)
    • Novelty (does it introduce new perspectives?)
    • Accessibility (is it open to all users?)
  • Reduces "rich-get-richer" effects (where popular items stay on top indefinitely).

Actionable Steps for Other Libraries: ✅ Avoid pure "click-based" ranking—it reinforces existing biases. ✅ Use "fair ranking" algorithms (e.g., Counterfactual Fairness, Equalized Odds). ✅ Test with real users—do diverse groups find the same results?

Strategy 4: Improve Metadata & Tagging for Underrepresented Content

What it is: Ensuring all materials—especially from marginalized groups—are properly indexed.

How Auraria Library Does It:

  • Trains staff on inclusive metadata practices (e.g., using controlled vocabularies for race, gender, disability).
  • Encourages authors to include diverse keywords (e.g., "Latinx studies" instead of just "Hispanic studies").
  • **Uses Linked Open Data (LOD) to connect related works across languages and cultures.

Actionable Steps for Other Libraries: ✅ Create a "metadata diversity checklist" before cataloging new items. ✅ Train librarians on inclusive tagging (e.g., avoiding "default white" assumptions). ✅ Use tools like Schema.org to standardize descriptions for better discovery.

Strategy 5: Foster Transparency in Algorithm Design

What it is: Making algorithm decisions explainable so users and stakeholders can challenge biases.

How Auraria Library Does It:

  • Publishes a "Library Algorithm Transparency Report" (annual).
  • Explains ranking factors (e.g., "We prioritize open-access materials 20% more to reduce paywall barriers").
  • Allows users to "opt out" of personalized recommendations if they prefer neutral results.

Actionable Steps for Other Libraries: ✅ Create a "plain-language" explanation of how algorithms work. ✅ Hold public workshops on algorithmic fairness. ✅ **Use XAI (Explainable AI) tools to break down decision-making.

Strategy 6: Partner with Marginalized Communities for Testing

What it is: Involving underrepresented users in testing and feedback loops.

How Auraria Library Does It:

  • Works with student groups (e.g., Black Student Union, Latinx Student Alliance) to test search results.
  • Conducts "bias testing sessions" where users flag problematic recommendations.
  • Adjusts systems based on feedback (e.g., adding more Arabic-language resources after student input).

Actionable Steps for Other Libraries: ✅ Form a "Library Algorithm Equity Task Force" with diverse members. ✅ Run "bias hunts" where users report unfair recommendations. ✅ Adjust policies based on community input.

Strategy 7: Reduce Dependency on Proprietary, Biased Vendors

What it is: Moving away from single-vendor discovery systems that may favor certain publishers.

How Auraria Library Does It:

  • Uses a mix of open-source and proprietary tools (e.g., Koha + EBSCO + WorldCat).
  • Negotiates with vendors to ensure fair representation of open-access and small presses.
  • Develops in-house solutions where possible (e.g., custom ILL workflows).

Actionable Steps for Other Libraries: ✅ Avoid "vendor lock-in"—diversify your tech stack. ✅ Push for "fair licensing" agreements that don’t favor big publishers. ✅ Explore open-source alternatives (e.g., Koha, Evergreen).
