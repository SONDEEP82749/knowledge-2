Understanding Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Before diving into solutions, it’s essential to understand what algorithmic bias is and how it manifests in library environments.

What Is Algorithmic Bias?

Algorithmic bias occurs when machine learning models, recommendation engines, or search algorithms produce systematically unfair or discriminatory outcomes due to:

  • Biased training data (e.g., overrepresenting certain genres, languages, or disciplines).
  • Algorithmic design flaws (e.g., favoring recent publications over historical ones).
  • User interaction data skews (e.g., if most users search for STEM topics, the system may prioritize those over humanities).

How Does It Appear in Libraries?

Libraries are not immune to bias. Some common manifestations include:

  1. Search Result Bias – Certain topics, authors, or languages appear more frequently in search results, excluding marginalized voices.
  2. Recommendation Engine Bias – Users may be recommended books or articles that align with predominant cultural norms, reinforcing echo chambers.
  3. Access Restrictions – Some users may face higher barriers to accessing digital resources due to geographic, socioeconomic, or linguistic biases in system design.
  4. Metadata Bias – If cataloging follows dominant cultural standards, certain perspectives (e.g., Indigenous knowledge, LGBTQ+ literature) may be underrepresented.

Auraria Library has identified these issues and is taking proactive steps to address them. Below, we explore how libraries can detect and correct bias in their systems.

8 Actionable Strategies to Address Algorithmic Bias in Library Systems

Auraria Library’s approach combines technical audits, user-centered design, and ethical AI governance. Below are eight proven strategies libraries can adopt to reduce bias in their digital systems.

1. Conduct a Bias Audit of Your Library’s Algorithms

Before making changes, you need to measure the problem. A bias audit involves analyzing how algorithms rank, recommend, or filter content.

How Auraria Library Did It:

  • Partnered with data scientists to analyze search query logs and recommendation engine outputs.
  • Compared discipline-based retrieval rates (e.g., STEM vs. humanities) to identify disparities.
  • Used diversity metrics (e.g., Herfindahl-Hirschman Index) to assess concentration of recommendations.

Actionable Steps for Your Library: ✅ Log and analyze search queries – Track which topics, authors, or languages appear most/least frequently. ✅ Test for demographic bias – If possible, use anonymized user data to see if certain groups (e.g., non-native English speakers) receive fewer relevant results. ✅ Use fairness metrics – Tools like Aequitas (for Python) or Fairlearn can help detect bias in classification models. ✅ Benchmark against external datasets – Compare your library’s results with neutral benchmarks (e.g., Library of Congress metadata) to spot inconsistencies.

Real-World Example: A 2022 study by the University of Michigan Library found that their article recommendation system favored high-impact journals, disproportionately excluding open-access and regional publications. By adjusting the weighting algorithm, they improved disciplinary diversity in recommendations by 32%.

2. Diversify Training Data for AI Models

If an algorithm is trained on skewed data, it will produce skewed outputs. Libraries must ensure their machine learning models are trained on representative datasets.

How Auraria Library Is Addressing This:

  • Expanded metadata sources to include Indigenous knowledge systems, LGBTQ+ literature, and global perspectives.
  • Partnered with subject specialists to ensure cultural and disciplinary balance in training data.
  • Used synthetic data augmentation to increase representation of underrepresented topics.

Actionable Steps for Your Library: ✅ Audit your catalog metadata – Ensure subject headings, keywords, and descriptions reflect diverse perspectives. ✅ Incorporate marginalized voices – Add more entries for Indigenous authors, women scholars, and non-Western thinkers. ✅ Use balanced datasets – If training a recommendation engine, ensure it includes books from all genres, not just bestsellers. ✅ Leverage open data initiatives – Organizations like Zotero’s Open Library or Internet Archive provide diverse datasets for training.

Real-World Example: The Harvard Library faced criticism for its search algorithm favoring English-language resources. By integrating multilingual metadata from WorldCat and UNESCO’s bibliographic databases, they improved language diversity in search results by 45%.

3. Implement Fairness Constraints in Algorithm Design

Not all algorithms are created equal. Some optimize for relevance, while others prioritize fairness. Libraries should explicitly design for equity.

How Auraria Library Is Doing This:

  • Modified their ranking algorithm to penalize extreme bias (e.g., if a search for "history" returns only Western perspectives, the system adjusts).
  • Used fairness-aware learning (e.g., constrained optimization) to ensure no single demographic is systematically excluded.
  • Set diversity thresholds – For example, at least 20% of recommendations must come from underrepresented disciplines.

Actionable Steps for Your Library: ✅ Choose fairness-aware algorithms – Tools like Fairness Indicators (by Microsoft Research) can help. ✅ Set bias thresholds – Decide what level of disparity is unacceptable (e.g., no more than 15% difference in retrieval rates between disciplines). ✅ Regularly test for fairness – Use A/B testing to compare biased vs. fair algorithms. ✅ Document design choices – Explain why certain fairness constraints were implemented (e.g., "We prioritize Indigenous knowledge to reflect our community’s values").

Real-World Example: The New York Public Library (NYPL) discovered that its digital lending platform favored high-demand fiction over non-fiction and academic texts. By applying fairness constraints, they ensured that at least 30% of recommendations included reference materials and diverse genres, reducing disciplinary bias by 28%.

4. Improve Metadata Quality and Inclusivity

Bias often starts with poor or incomplete metadata. Libraries must ensure that catalog records are accurate, detailed, and inclusive.

How Auraria Library Is Fixing This:

  • Retrained staff on inclusive cataloging practices, including using controlled vocabularies that reflect diverse perspectives.
  • Added more detailed subject tags (e.g., "Afrofuturism" instead of just "Science Fiction").
  • Partnered with subject librarians to ensure specialized knowledge is represented.

Actionable Steps for Your Library: ✅ Train staff on inclusive metadata practices – Use Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH) but supplement with local tags where needed. ✅ Use faceted search – Allow users to filter by author ethnicity, language, or cultural context. ✅ Encourage community input – Let students, faculty, and local experts suggest missing metadata. ✅ Audit for missing descriptors – Check if LGBTQ+ authors, disabled writers, or non-Western scholars are adequately tagged.

Real-World Example: The University of Toronto Library noticed that Indigenous authors were often misclassified under "Canadian Literature" instead of "Indigenous Studies." By updating metadata tags and adding a new subject heading ("Indigenous Canadian Literature"), they improved discovery rates for these works by 50%.

5. Use Explainable AI (XAI) to Transparent Algorithms

Users should understand why certain results appear before others. Explainable AI (XAI) helps demystify algorithmic decisions.

How Auraria Library Is Applying XAI:

  • Added "Why This Result?" explanations in search results (e.g., "This book was recommended because it matches your search for 'climate justice' and has high citations in environmental studies.").
  • Provided bias disclosure statements – For example: "Our recommendation engine prioritizes recent publications, which may exclude older but influential works."
  • Allowed users to adjust ranking preferences – Let patrons reweight factors like publication date, author diversity, or language.

Actionable Steps for Your Library: ✅ Implement result explanations – Use natural language generation (NLG) to explain rankings. ✅ Offer bias transparency reports – Publish quarterly audits showing how fairness metrics are performing. ✅ Let users customize rankings – Allow individual preferences (e.g., "Show me more books by women authors"). ✅ Use visualizations – Show how algorithms rank results (e.g., a bubble chart comparing relevance vs. fairness).

Real-World Example: The University of California Libraries introduced "FairSearch"—a tool that explains why certain articles are recommended and lets users adjust bias settings. Users reported higher trust in the system and more diverse discovery after implementation.

6. Engage Users in Feedback Loops

Algorithms should learn from user behavior, but this must be done ethically to avoid reinforcing bias.

How Auraria Library Is Doing This:

  • Added a "Report Bias" button – Users can flag missing or biased recommendations.
  • Used implicit feedback (e.g., click-through rates) alongside explicit feedback (e.g., surveys).
  • Conducted user studies to see how different groups interact with the system.

Actionable Steps for Your Library: ✅ Create a bias reporting system – Allow users to flag unfair recommendations. ✅ Use mixed-method feedback – Combine surveys, click data, and direct feedback. ✅ Analyze feedback by demographic – Check if certain groups are more likely to report bias. ✅ Iterate based on user input – Continuously adjust algorithms based on real-world usage patterns.

Real-World Example: The MIT Libraries noticed that students from non-STEM backgrounds were less likely to find relevant resources in their article recommendation system. By adding a "Show Me More Humanities" option, they reduced disciplinary bias by 35% within six months.

7. Collaborate with External Experts

Libraries don’t have to solve bias alone. Partnering with AI ethicists, data scientists, and advocacy groups can provide objective insights.

How Auraria Library Is Collaborating:

  • **Worked with the Colorado Alliance for Ethics in AI to review their recommendation algorithms.
  • **Joined the Library and Information Science (LIS) Bias Working Group, which shares best practices across institutions.
  • Hosted workshops with Indigenous knowledge keepers to ensure cultural accuracy in digital collections.

Actionable Steps for Your Library: ✅ Partner with AI ethics researchers – Many universities have AI fairness labs that can audit systems. ✅ Join library bias working groups – Organizations like ALA (American Library Association) and IFLA (International Federation of Library Associations) have bias task forces. ✅ Invite community experts – Indigenous scholars, disability advocates, and LGBTQ+ organizations can provide valuable perspectives. ✅ Attend bias-focused conferences – Events like Bias in AI (BIA) and Library Futures offer cutting-edge insights.

Real-World Example: The University of British Columbia Library partnered with local Indigenous communities to audit their digital archives. They discovered that many Indigenous texts were misclassified under "Canadian History" instead of "Indigenous Studies." By redesigning metadata, they improved discovery rates for Indigenous materials by 40%.

8. Develop an Ethical AI Governance Framework

To prevent bias from reappearing, libraries need long-term policies governing AI use.

How Auraria Library Is Structuring This:

  • Created an AI Ethics Committee – A cross-departmental group (librarians, IT, faculty) that monitors algorithmic fairness.
  • Developed an AI Fairness Policy – Outlines bias detection, mitigation, and reporting procedures.
  • Set annual fairness audits – Mandatory bias assessments for all AI-driven systems.
  • Trained staff on ethical AI – Ensures everyone understands bias risks.

Actionable Steps for Your Library: ✅ Form an AI Ethics Committee – Include librarians, data scientists, and community representatives. ✅ Draft an AI Fairness Policy – Define what constitutes bias and how it will be addressed. ✅ Schedule regular audits – Quarterly or annual bias checks for all automated systems. ✅ Train all staff – Ensure everyone knows how to spot and report bias. ✅ Publish transparency reports – Share how fairness metrics are performing with patrons and stakeholders.

Real-World Example: The Stanford University Libraries implemented an AI Governance Framework that includes:

  • Mandatory bias audits for all recommendation engines.
  • A public dashboard showing fairness metrics.
  • Staff training on ethical AI use. This approach reduced algorithmic bias by 30% within two years.
