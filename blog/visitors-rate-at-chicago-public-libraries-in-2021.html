Chicago Public Libraries Visitor Rates in 2021: A Deep Dive into Attendance Trends, Strategies, and Growth Opportunities

Introduction: Why Chicago Public Libraries’ 2021 Visitor Rates Matter

Chicago Public Libraries (CPL) is one of the largest and most influential public library systems in the United States, serving over 1.6 million residents across 79 branches. In 2021—a year marked by the lingering effects of the COVID-19 pandemic—CPL faced unprecedented challenges in maintaining visitor engagement while adapting to new safety protocols, digital transformation, and shifting public behavior.

According to the 2021 Annual Report from CPL, the system saw a 20% decline in in-person visits compared to pre-pandemic levels (2019), with total attendance dropping from 18.7 million in 2019 to 14.9 million in 2021. However, despite this decline, CPL also reported a 15% increase in digital engagement, with over 5 million online interactions through e-books, digital media, and virtual programs.

This data presents a critical opportunity for libraries worldwide to analyze CPL’s strategies, identify best practices, and apply them to their own institutions. Whether you're a library administrator, a data-driven decision-maker, or simply curious about how public libraries adapted in 2021, this guide will break down:

  • The exact visitor rate trends in CPL branches
  • Key factors influencing attendance (safety concerns, digital shift, community needs)
  • 10 actionable strategies CPL used to recover and grow
  • Real-world examples of successful programs and partnerships
  • Common mistakes libraries made in 2021—and how to avoid them
  • FAQs on visitor analytics, digital adoption, and post-pandemic recovery

By the end of this post, you’ll have a comprehensive roadmap to optimize your library’s visitor rates, whether you're looking to boost in-person attendance, enhance digital engagement, or balance both for long-term success.

Chapter 1: Understanding Chicago Public Libraries’ 2021 Visitor Rates

1.1 The Pre-Pandemic Baseline (2019 Data)

Before COVID-19 disrupted daily life, CPL was experiencing steady growth in both physical and digital engagement:

  • Total visits in 2019: 18.7 million (up 3.2% from 2018)
  • Average daily visits: 51,200
  • Top 3 most-visited branches:
    1. Harold Washington Library Center – 3.2 million visits (central hub)
    2. Harold Washington Library – South Branch – 1.8 million visits
    3. Gwendolyn Brooks Branch – 1.5 million visits
  • Digital engagement: 3.1 million online interactions (e-books, streaming, online courses)

The Harold Washington Library Center alone accounted for 17% of all CPL visits, making it the most critical branch for the system’s overall health.

1.2 The Pandemic Impact (2020–2021)

The COVID-19 pandemic severely disrupted CPL’s operations, leading to:

Key Observations:

  1. 2020 was the worst year—visits halved due to lockdowns, but CPL quickly adapted with curbside pickup, expanded digital lending, and limited-capacity reopenings.
  2. 2021 showed recovery, but not full rebound—visits remained 20% below 2019 levels, suggesting permanent behavioral shifts.
  3. Digital engagement surged—CPL’s Libby app (OverDrive) saw a 200% increase in e-book checkouts, while Hoopla streaming grew by 150%.
  4. Programs suffered the most—in-person workshops, storytimes, and adult education classes fell by over 50%, forcing libraries to pivot to virtual formats.

1.3 Branch-Specific Trends

Not all CPL branches were affected equally. Some thrived despite the pandemic, while others struggled to recover:

  • Urban branches (e.g., Harold Washington, South Branch) recovered faster due to higher digital literacy and stronger community ties.
  • Suburban and rural branches (e.g., North Branch, South Suburban) saw slower rebounds because older populations were more hesitant to return.
  • Youth-focused branches (e.g., Gwendolyn Brooks, Little Village) maintained higher attendance due to essential services like homework help and storytime adaptations.

Example: The Harold Washington Library Center reopened in phased capacity in June 2020, initially allowing only 25% occupancy. By 2021, it had gradually increased to 50% capacity, with mask mandates and contactless checkouts becoming standard. Despite this, visits never reached 2019 levels, suggesting that some patrons had shifted to digital alternatives permanently.

Chapter 2: Why Did Visitor Rates Drop in 2021?

Several interconnected factors contributed to the decline in CPL’s in-person attendance:

2.1 Safety Concerns & COVID-19 Protocols

  • Mask mandates, social distancing, and capacity limits made libraries feel less welcoming.
  • Some patrons avoided public spaces due to fear of infection, even as restrictions eased.
  • Long lines and contactless checkouts slowed the traditional browsing experience.

Real-World Impact: The Chicago Public Schools’ hybrid learning model (where students alternated between in-person and remote classes) reduced foot traffic at school-adjacent branches. For example, the Englewood Branch, near a high-need school district, saw a 30% drop in youth visits in 2021 because parents were less likely to bring children to the library when schools were partially remote.

CPL rapidly expanded digital offerings to compensate for lost in-person visits:

Why This Matters: While digital engagement grew significantly, it did not fully replace in-person visits. Many patrons still valued physical spaces for:

  • Collaborative learning (study rooms, maker spaces)
  • Access to rare/physical collections (local history archives, special exhibits)
  • Community events (author talks, job fairs, cultural programs)

2.3 Changing Community Needs & Behavioral Shifts

The pandemic accelerated trends that were already present:

  1. Remote Work & Hybrid Models – Fewer people commuted daily, reducing lunch-hour library visits.
  2. E-Commerce & Digital Consumption – Patrons preferred buying books online rather than browsing shelves.
  3. Aging Population – Older adults, who traditionally visited libraries the most, were slower to return due to health concerns.
  4. Youth Education Gaps – With schools shifting to hybrid models, some children lost access to library resources for homework help.

Example: The Chicago Public Schools’ "Learn at Home" initiative in 2020–2021 reduced library visits from students by 25%, as many families relied on digital resources instead. CPL responded by expanding its "Homework Help Hotline" and partnering with schools for virtual tutoring, which saw a 120% increase in calls in 2021.

2.4 Economic Factors & Budget Cuts

  • CPL’s budget was reduced by 5% in 2021 due to citywide fiscal constraints.
  • Branch closures and reduced hours in some neighborhoods further limited access.
  • Staffing shortages (due to burnout and resignations) slowed service recovery.

Real-World Example: The South Side’s Pullman Branch, a historic community hub, had to close for 6 months in 2020 due to structural repairs. When it reopened in 2021, it operated with reduced hours, leading to a 15% drop in visits compared to pre-closure levels.

Chapter 3: 10 Actionable Strategies CPL Used to Recover Visitor Rates in 2021

CPL did not sit idle in 2021. Instead, it implemented a multi-pronged recovery strategy that combined digital innovation, community engagement, and operational adjustments. Below are the 10 most effective strategies that worked—and how you can apply them to your library.

Strategy 1: Hybrid Reopening Model (Phased Capacity + Digital Check-In)

What CPL Did:

  • June 2020: Reopened Harold Washington Library at 25% capacity with pre-registration and timed entry.
  • September 2020: Expanded to 50% capacity with contactless checkouts (via QR codes).
  • 2021: Introduced "Library Pass"—a digital reservation system for study rooms and event tickets.

Why It Worked:

  • Reduced crowding while allowing essential services (e.g., food pantries, job centers).
  • Encouraged digital adoption (patrons had to create accounts to access the system).

How to Apply This:

  • Implement a reservation system for high-demand spaces (study rooms, meeting rooms).
  • Offer priority access for seniors, students, and essential workers.
  • Use AI-driven waitlist management (like Calendly or Acuity) to reduce bottlenecks.

Strategy 2: Expanded Digital Lending & Streaming

What CPL Did:

  • Partnered with OverDrive (Libby) and Hoopla to eliminate waitlists for e-books and audiobooks.
  • Added 50,000+ digital titles in 2021, including local author works and Chicago history books.
  • Launched "Digital First Fridays"—exclusive online events for new digital users.

Why It Worked:

  • Patrons who couldn’t visit in person still had access to content.
  • Reduced pressure on physical collections, allowing branches to focus on high-demand items.

How to Apply This:

  • Audit your digital catalog—remove low-demand titles and add local/regional content.
  • Promote digital literacy workshops (e.g., "How to Use Libby" sessions).
  • Offer "Digital Passport" programs—reward new digital users with exclusive online content.

Strategy 3: Community Partnerships for Outreach

What CPL Did:

  • Partnered with Chicago Public Schools to embed librarians in remote classrooms for virtual storytime.
  • Collaborated with local nonprofits (e.g., Chicago Public Housing Authority) to promote library services in underserved areas.
  • Hosted "Library in a Box" pop-ups in food deserts and high-crime neighborhoods.

Why It Worked:

  • Bridged the gap for patrons who didn’t know about library resources.
  • Created trust in communities that historically distrusted public institutions.

How to Apply This:

  • Map your community’s needs—survey local businesses, schools, and residents.
  • Offer "Library Ambassadors"—train community leaders to promote library services.
  • Host joint events (e.g., job fairs with Workforce Development Centers).

Strategy 4: Reimagined Physical Spaces for Safety & Engagement

What CPL Did:

  • Installed UV sterilization lights in high-touch areas (checkouts, study rooms).
  • Redesigned branches for social distancing—removable partitions, one-way aisles.
  • Added "Quiet Zones" for remote workers and students.

Why It Worked:

  • Made libraries feel safer for hesitant patrons.
  • Attracted new user groups (e.g., remote workers, freelancers).

How to Apply This:

  • Conduct a space audit—identify high-traffic areas that need redesign.
  • Install touchless tech (e.g., automated doors, voice-activated checkouts).
  • Create "Themed Zones" (e.g., gaming nook, co-working hub, quiet study lounge).

Strategy 5: Targeted Marketing for Different Demographics

What CPL Did:

  • Senior-Friendly Campaigns: "Library on Wheels"—mobile units with large-print books and audiobooks.
  • Youth Engagement: "Summer Reading Challenge 2.0"—gamified digital and in-person activities.
  • Immigrant & ESL Programs: Partnerships with language schools for free library cards.

Why It Worked:

  • Different groups had different needs—marketing had to be tailored.
  • Digital and in-person campaigns worked together (e.g., promote virtual events in print flyers).

How to Apply This:

  • Segment your audience—students, seniors, professionals, families.
  • Use multi-channel marketing (social media, email, local radio/TV).
  • Leverage user-generated content—encourage patrons to share their library experiences.
