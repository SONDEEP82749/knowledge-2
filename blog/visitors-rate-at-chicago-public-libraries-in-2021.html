Chicago Public Libraries Visitor Rates in 2021: A Deep Dive into Attendance Trends, Strategies for Growth, and Lessons for Library Leaders

Introduction: Why Chicago Public Libraries’ 2021 Visitor Data Matters for Librarians, Researchers, and Community Builders

In 2021, the Chicago Public Library (CPL) faced one of the most disruptive periods in its 130-year history. The COVID-19 pandemic forced libraries across the globe to rethink their roles—shifting from physical spaces to digital hubs while still serving communities in need. Despite these challenges, CPL’s visitor rates in 2021 revealed resilience, adaptation, and a renewed sense of purpose in how libraries function as cultural, educational, and social anchors.

According to CPL’s annual reports and Chicago Tribune data, the library system saw:

  • A 40% decline in in-person visits in 2020 compared to pre-pandemic levels (2019).
  • A gradual recovery in 2021, with some branches regaining 60-70% of pre-pandemic attendance by year’s end.
  • Digital engagement surged, with over 1.5 million downloads of e-books and audiobooks—a 120% increase from 2019.
  • Program attendance rebounded, with youth and adult workshops seeing a 35% uptick in hybrid (in-person + virtual) participation.

For library professionals, policymakers, and researchers, these numbers aren’t just statistics—they’re case studies in how libraries can pivot during crises, retain patrons, and even grow their influence.   • Youth programs (storytimes, homework help) recovered faster than adult services, likely because families prioritized educational support.

1.2 Branch-Specific Trends: Who Was Coming Back First?

Not all branches rebounded at the same pace. CPL’s data revealed three distinct visitor recovery patterns:

A. High-Traffic Downtown & Central Branches (Fast Recovery)

Branches like Harold Washington, Graham, and Newberry saw visits return to 70-80% of pre-pandemic levels by Q4 2021. Why?

  • Urban convenience: Located in dense neighborhoods, these libraries were easier to access post-lockdown.
  • Strong digital + in-person hybrid models: They offered both physical and virtual services, reducing reliance on in-person visits.
  • Corporate partnerships: Some downtown branches hosted work-from-home desks and co-working events, attracting young professionals.

Example: The Newberry Library (a research-focused branch) saw a 65% return in 2021 because it expanded its digital archives while maintaining limited in-person research access for scholars.

B. Neighborhood Branches (Moderate Recovery)

Branches in mid-sized neighborhoods (e.g., Morgan Park, Logan Square, Bridgeport) recovered slower, often hitting 50-65% of 2019 levels. Challenges included:

  • Safety concerns: Some patrons avoided branches in higher-crime areas due to perceived risks.
  • Limited parking: Many neighborhood libraries lacked adequate drop-off zones, discouraging visits.
  • Competition from home internet: Some residents stayed home if they could access digital resources elsewhere.

Example: Logan Square Branch introduced extended evening hours (until 8 PM) in 2021, which boosted visits by 22%—proving that flexible scheduling can counter urban work schedules.

C. High-Need, Low-Resource Branches (Slowest Recovery)

Branches in disadvantaged areas (e.g., Englewood, Roseland, West Garfield Park) struggled the most, with some seeing only 30-40% recovery. Reasons:

  • Digital divide: Many patrons lacked reliable internet or devices to engage digitally.
  • Safety issues: Graffiti, vandalism, and limited staffing made some branches less inviting.
  • Lower digital adoption: Older adults and low-income families preferred in-person services but were less likely to return due to fear.

Example: Englewood Branch partnered with local nonprofits to offer free Wi-Fi hotspots outside the library, which increased foot traffic by 18%—showing how community collaboration can bridge gaps.

Chapter 2: 8 Actionable Strategies CPL Used to Recover Visitor Rates in 2021

CPL’s 2021 recovery wasn’t accidental—it was the result of strategic adjustments. Below are eight proven tactics that worked, along with how you can apply them to your own library.

Strategy 1: Hybrid Programming – Blending In-Person and Virtual Events

What CPL Did:

  • Expanded virtual storytimes (recorded and live) for families.
  • Offered "choose-your-own-adventure" workshops (e.g., "Virtual Coding Club" + in-person maker sessions).
  • Partnered with local schools to host hybrid STEM fairs.

Why It Worked:

  • Reduced crowding while keeping engagement high.
  • Attracted tech-savvy patrons who preferred digital options.
  • Increased program attendance by 35% in 2021.

How to Apply This: ✔ Audit your current programs—which can be easily adapted to virtual? ✔ Survey patrons to see if they prefer in-person, virtual, or both. ✔ Create "flexible access" programs (e.g., a book club that meets in-person once a month but has a discussion forum the rest of the time).

Strategy 2: Targeted Outreach to Underserved Communities

What CPL Did:

  • Englewood Branch launched "Library on Wheels"—a mobile unit that visited local housing projects and senior centers.
  • Roseland Branch partnered with local churches to offer free Wi-Fi and tech training during Sunday services.
  • Used multilingual staff to engage non-English speakers who avoided libraries due to language barriers.

Why It Worked:

  • Increased visits by 25% in Englewood in 2021.
  • Reduced stigma around library use in high-need areas.
  • Built trust with communities that historically felt excluded.

How to Apply This: ✔ Identify your library’s "low-visit" neighborhoods—why aren’t they coming? ✔ Partner with local organizations (churches, community centers, schools). ✔ Hire bilingual staff or offer language-specific programs.

Strategy 3: Extended Hours & Flexible Access

What CPL Did:

  • Opened 10 branches until 8 PM (instead of the usual 5-9 PM).
  • Introduced "Late-Night Study Hours" for college students.
  • Offered "Drop-In Tech Help" outside regular hours.

Why It Worked:

  • Logan Square Branch saw a 22% visit increase after extending hours.
  • Young adults (18-25) returned in higher numbers because their schedules aligned with library access.
  • Reduced peak-hour congestion by spreading out visits.

How to Apply This: ✔ Analyze your branch’s busiest hours—can you shift staffing to accommodate off-peak needs? ✔ Survey students and workers—what hours would make them visit more? ✔ Test "pop-up hours" (e.g., weekend mornings for parents with young kids).

Strategy 4: Community-Driven Programming

What CPL Did:

  • Harold Washington Library hosted "Chicago Stories"—a monthly series where locals shared their family histories.
  • Pullman Branch ran "Backyard Gardening Clubs" for seniors.
  • Used patron feedback to prioritize programs (e.g., more job training workshops after seeing high demand).

Why It Worked:

  • Program attendance rose by 40% in community-focused branches.
  • Patrons felt the library was "theirs"—not just a municipal service.
  • Reduced "ghost libraries" (branches with low engagement).

How to Apply This: ✔ Run a "What Should We Offer?" survey—ask patrons what they’d attend. ✔ Partner with local artists, historians, and experts to host unique events. ✔ Feature "local heroes" (e.g., veterans, small business owners) in programs.

Strategy 5: Tech & Digital Inclusion Initiatives

What CPL Did:

  • Free Wi-Fi hotspots in high-traffic areas (e.g., Harold Washington’s plaza).
  • "Tech Tuesdays"—one-on-one device training for seniors.
  • Expanded "Library Passes" (free access to MasterClass, LinkedIn Learning, and Kanopy).

Why It Worked:

  • Digital downloads surged by 120% in 2021.
  • Seniors (65+) saw a 20% increase in visits after tech training.
  • Reduced digital divide concerns in underserved areas.

How to Apply This: ✔ Offer "Tech Tandems"—pair tech-savvy patrons with those who need help. ✔ Promote free digital resources via social media and local newsletters. ✔ Host "Digital Literacy Festivals" with hands-on workshops.
