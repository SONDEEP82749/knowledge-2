Chicago Public Libraries Visitor Rates in 2021: A Deep Dive into Attendance Trends, Strategies, and Growth Opportunities

Introduction: Understanding the Impact of COVID-19 on Chicago Public Libraries in 2021

The Chicago Public Library (CPL) system is one of the largest and most influential public library networks in the United States, serving over 1.7 million residents across 78 branches. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, CPL was a bustling hub of knowledge, community engagement, and digital innovation, with annual visitor rates exceeding 12 million in 2019 (Chicago Public Library Annual Report, 2019).

However, the pandemic disrupted this trajectory dramatically. In 2020, CPL reported a 60% drop in physical visits, with many branches operating under limited capacity or closing temporarily. By 2021, as vaccination rates rose and safety protocols eased, the library system began its recovery—but not without challenges.

This blog post explores Chicago Public Libraries’ visitor rates in 2021, analyzing:

  • How attendance rebounded after pandemic restrictions
  • Key strategies that helped CPL regain foot traffic
  • Lessons learned from digital engagement and hybrid services
  • Common mistakes libraries made—and how to avoid them
  • Actionable tips for other public libraries looking to boost attendance

By the end, you’ll have a comprehensive understanding of how CPL navigated the post-pandemic landscape—and how your own library can apply similar strategies.

Chicago Public Libraries’ Visitor Trends in 2021: A Year of Recovery and Adaptation

1. The Pre-Pandemic Benchmark (2019)

Before COVID-19, Chicago Public Libraries saw consistent growth in both physical and digital engagement:

  • Total visits: 12.3 million (2019)
  • Average daily visitors: ~34,000
  • Most visited branch: Harry A. Bell Branch (Downtown) with over 1 million visits annually
  • Digital engagement: 5.2 million online interactions (including e-books, research databases, and virtual programs)

The library system was already expanding its digital offerings, recognizing the shift toward online resources. However, nothing could have prepared them for the abrupt shutdown in March 2020.

2. The Pandemic Slump (2020)

When the pandemic hit, CPL closed all branches by mid-March 2020. By June, some locations reopened with limited capacity, but attendance remained severely depressed:

  • Total visits in 2020: ~4.9 million (a 60% decline)
  • Peak recovery month: October 2020 (when some branches reopened at 50% capacity) saw ~1.2 million visits
  • Digital surge: Online checkouts increased by 300%, with e-books and audiobooks becoming the most accessed resources.

The shift to remote learning and work forced CPL to pivot quickly—offering curbside pickup, expanded digital lending, and virtual programs to keep the community engaged.

3. The 2021 Recovery: A Mixed Picture

In 2021, CPL faced two major challenges:

  1. Reopening safely while balancing public health and accessibility.
  2. Retaining digital users who had grown accustomed to online services.

By June 2021, all branches were fully operational, but attendance numbers remained below pre-pandemic levels:

  • Total visits in 2021: ~8.7 million (a 29% recovery from 2020, but still 29% below 2019)
  • Highest-visited branch: Harry A. Bell Branch (recovered to ~800,000 visits)
  • Digital engagement remained strong: 4.8 million online interactions (slightly down from 2020’s peak)

Key takeaway: While CPL made progress, not all visitors returned immediately. Some patrons preferred digital convenience, while others struggled with re-adjusting to in-person services.

8 Actionable Strategies That Helped CPL Boost Visitor Rates in 2021

Chicago Public Libraries didn’t just wait for recovery—they actively implemented strategies to attract visitors back. Here are the most effective tactics they used, along with real-world examples and how other libraries can apply them.

Strategy 1: Hybrid Programming – Blending In-Person and Virtual Events

Why it worked: The pandemic proved that flexibility is key. CPL realized that not all patrons wanted to return to physical spaces immediately, so they expanded hybrid events—allowing people to attend in-person or online.

How CPL executed it:

  • "Storytime Anywhere" – A series of live virtual storytimes that also aired on YouTube and Facebook, with in-person sessions for those who preferred physical attendance.
  • "Tech Tuesdays" – Weekly in-person and virtual workshops on digital literacy, coding, and job search tools, ensuring accessibility for all.
  • "Author Talks in Two Formats" – Some events were streamed live, while others allowed in-person attendance with social distancing.

Real-world example: The Chicago Public Library’s "Music & Words" series featured local poets and musicians performing both online and in select branches. This approach doubled engagement, with 3,200 virtual attendees and 1,800 in-person visitors across three events.

How your library can apply this: ✅ Offer "choose-your-own-adventure" events—let patrons decide between in-person or virtual. ✅ Record and repurpose virtual events for on-demand viewing. ✅ Partner with local artists and speakers to create exclusive hybrid experiences.

Strategy 2: Curbside Pickup & Expanded Digital Lending

Why it worked: Many patrons didn’t want to risk exposure in crowded libraries, so CPL simplified access with contactless services.

How CPL executed it:

  • Curbside pickup was permanent (not just a pandemic measure), allowing patrons to request items online and pick them up without entering the library.
  • Expanded e-book and audiobook collections—CPL partnered with OverDrive and Libby to increase digital titles by 40% in 2021.
  • "Library Passport" – A digital membership that allowed unlimited e-book checkouts without visiting a branch.

Real-world example: The Skokie Public Library (a CPL partner system) saw a 250% increase in e-book checkouts after implementing "Passport Perks", a program that gave free digital access to all residents. CPL later adopted a similar model, leading to 150,000+ digital checkouts in Q1 2021 alone.

How your library can apply this: ✅ Make curbside pickup a permanent option—some patrons will never return to in-person browsing. ✅ Increase digital collections—partner with Libby, Hoopla, or Kanopy to offer more free content. ✅ Create "digital-first" membership tiers to attract remote users.

Strategy 3: Reimagining Physical Spaces for Social Distancing & Innovation

Why it worked: Libraries couldn’t just reopen and expect the same crowds. CPL redesigned branch layouts to encourage spacing, hygiene, and new uses.

How CPL executed it:

  • "Library as a Hub" – Some branches became community centers for job fairs, vaccine clinics, and food pantries, drawing new visitors.
  • Modular seating & plexiglass barriers – Harry A. Bell Branch installed adjustable tables and clear dividers to reduce crowding.
  • "Quiet Zones" for focus – Many branches designated silent areas for remote workers and students, attracting new demographics.

Real-world example: The Pullman Branch (a historic, high-traffic location) rebranded as a "Community Innovation Lab" in 2021. By offering 3D printing workshops, maker space access, and small business consultations, they increased visits by 35% compared to 2020.

How your library can apply this: ✅ Repurpose underused spaces—turn a quiet corner into a co-working zone or parenting resource center. ✅ Install flexible furniture—modular tables and acoustic panels can adapt to different needs. ✅ Host "pop-up" events in less crowded branches to distribute foot traffic.

Strategy 4: Targeted Marketing to Re-Engage Lost Patrons

Why it worked: Not all visitors returned automatically. CPL used data-driven marketing to reconnect with lapsed patrons.

How CPL executed it:

  • "Welcome Back" email campaigns – Sent to inactive members with exclusive offers (e.g., free virtual book clubs, priority curbside pickup).
  • Social media challenges – "#ReadChicago2021" encouraged book clubs and individuals to share their reads, driving organic engagement.
  • Partnerships with local businesses – Starbucks, Barnes & Noble, and local bookstores promoted library passes and events.

Real-world example: The "Summer of Stories" campaign in 2021 targeted parents and educators with free virtual author visits and reading challenges. The campaign doubled participation compared to 2020, with 12,000+ downloads of reading logs.

How your library can apply this: ✅ Segment your patron list—target inactive members with personalized offers. ✅ Leverage local influencers—partner with book bloggers, teachers, and community leaders. ✅ Run referral programs—offer discounts or prizes for bringing new visitors.

Strategy 5: Expanding STEM & Career Services to Attract New Audiences

Why it worked: The pandemic accelerated remote work and online learning, so CPL invested in career and tech resources to attract younger and working-age patrons.

How CPL executed it:

  • "Career Launch" workshops – Free resume writing, interview prep, and LinkedIn optimization sessions.
  • Tech training for seniors – "Tech Tuesdays for All Ages" helped older adults navigate Zoom, email, and online banking.
  • Maker spaces & 3D printing – Branches like Pullman and Newberry offered free access to design tools, attracting students and entrepreneurs.

Real-world example: The "Future Ready" initiative at Chicago Public Library’s Newberry Branch saw 4,000+ participants in 2021 career workshops. Many attendees were unemployed or underemployed, and 60% reported job offers or promotions within six months.

How your library can apply this: ✅ Offer "career sprints"—intensive, short-term workshops on high-demand skills. ✅ Partner with local businesses to host job fairs in your library. ✅ Create "tech mentorship" programs—pair seniors with younger patrons for digital skill-sharing.

Strategy 6: Community Partnerships to Extend Reach

Why it worked: CPL realized they couldn’t grow alone. By collaborating with schools, nonprofits, and businesses, they drew in new visitors.

How CPL executed it:

  • "Library in a Box" – Mobile units visited shelters, senior centers, and low-income neighborhoods, offering books, Wi-Fi, and digital literacy classes.
  • Partnership with CPS (Chicago Public Schools) – Free library passes for all students, plus homework help and book clubs in schools.
  • Collaboration with "Chicago Public Schools’ Office of Equity" – Bilingual storytimes and ESL resources in high-need communities.

Real-world example: The "Library on Wheels" program in Englewood and Roseland saw 2,500+ visits in its first six months, with many first-time library users signing up for digital accounts.

How your library can apply this: ✅ Launch a mobile library unit—even a van or trailer can reach underserved areas. ✅ Partner with schools to bring library resources into classrooms. ✅ Work with nonprofits to host joint events (e.g., food drives + book distributions).

Strategy 7: Data-Driven Decision Making (Using Analytics to Improve Services)

Why it worked: CPL tracked patron behavior to identify what was working and what needed adjustment.

How CPL executed it:

  • Heatmaps of branch usage – Harry A. Bell Branch noticed peak hours (3-5 PM) and adjusted staffing accordingly.
  • Surveys & feedback forms – Asked patrons why they weren’t returning, leading to more flexible hours and digital options.
  • A/B testing event formats – Some storytimes were live-streamed, others recorded—data showed live events had higher engagement.

Real-world example: By analyzing check-out data, CPL discovered that YA (Young Adult) fiction was the most borrowed genre in 2021. They expanded YA collections by 20% and hosted more teen-focused events, leading to a 15% increase in teen visits.

How your library can apply this: ✅ Use free tools like Google Analytics to track website and event performance. ✅ Send post-event surveys to understand what patrons want. ✅ Adjust hours based on data—if mornings are slow, offer "quiet hours" for remote workers.

Why it worked: Social media is a powerful tool for driving foot traffic. CPL designed branches and events that encouraged sharing.

  • "Library Scavenger Hunts" – Hidden books, QR codes, and challenges in branches like Newberry and Pullman encouraged visitors to post photos.
  • Themed "Book Nooks" – Decorated reading corners (e.g., Harry Potter-themed, sci-fi futuristic) became popular photo ops.
  • "Library Challenges" – "Read 12 Books in 12 Months" and "Bookstagram Takeovers" where local book influencers hosted events.
