8 Actionable Strategies to Make the Most of Wake County’s Free Tutoring

Now that you know how the program works, let’s explore how to use it effectively to maximize your learning outcomes.

Strategy 1: Schedule Consistent Tutoring Sessions

Problem: Many students sign up but only use tutoring when they’re desperate—leading to last-minute cramming and poor retention.

Solution:

  • Block out 1–2 hours per week for tutoring, just like a regular class.
  • Set specific goals (e.g., "I’ll improve my algebra grade by one letter" or "I’ll master 5 chemistry formulas").
  • Track progress in a notebook or digital spreadsheet to stay accountable.

Example: *A Wake County junior, Jamie, struggled with biology. Instead of waiting until the night before the test, she scheduled weekly 30-minute sessions with a tutor. By the end of the semester, she went from a C- to an A-.*

Strategy 2: Prepare Questions Before Each Session

Problem: Walking into a tutoring session with no clear questions wastes time and money (even if it’s free).

Solution:

  • List 3–5 specific questions before each session (e.g., "Can you explain how to balance chemical equations?").
  • Bring your textbook, notes, or past assignments so the tutor can reference them.
  • Use the "5 Whys" technique—if you’re stuck on a problem, ask why you don’t understand it, then dig deeper.

Example: *A college freshman, Marcus, would show up to tutoring with vague questions like, "I don’t get this physics problem." His tutor helped him break it down step-by-step, leading to A’s in his next exam.

Strategy 3: Use Asynchronous Help for Quick Doubts

Problem: Not everyone can schedule live sessions due to busy schedules or time zones.

Solution:

  • Submit questions anytime via the library’s tutoring portal.
  • Get responses within 24 hours—great for last-minute homework help.
  • Save past answers for future reference.

Example: *A homeschooled teen, Sophia, would submit math problems at 10 PM and get answers by morning. This helped her stay ahead in her curriculum without extra stress.

Strategy 4: Leverage Test Prep Resources

Problem: Many students crash-course for exams without structured prep.

Solution:

  • Use the library’s SAT/ACT practice tests to identify weak areas.
  • Focus on one subject per week (e.g., "This week, I’ll master reading comprehension").

Strategy 5: Join Study Groups via Tutoring

Problem: Some students learn better in a group setting but don’t know where to find peers.

Solution:

  • Ask your tutor to connect you with other students in the same subject.
  • Use the library’s online forums to discuss problems.

Example: *A group of AP Biology students in Wake County formed a weekly study group using the library’s tutoring chat. They all improved their grades by 20%+ through collaborative learning.

Strategy 6: Use Tutoring for Career & Soft Skills

Problem: Many students only think of tutoring for schoolwork, missing out on career-boosting opportunities.

Solution:

  • Get resume reviews from career tutors.

Strategy 7: Combine Tutoring with Self-Study

Problem: Relying only on tutors can make learning passive rather than active.

Solution:

  • Watch tutorial videos (Khan Academy, Crash Course) before sessions.
  • Practice problems independently and ask tutors to check your work.
  • Teach concepts to someone else (even if it’s just explaining it aloud).

Example: *A college student, Emily, would watch YouTube lectures on statistics before tutoring. She’d then ask her tutor to quiz her on the material, leading to stronger retention.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Using Online Tutoring

While Wake County’s tutoring program is incredibly valuable, some students don’t maximize its potential due to bad habits. Here’s how to avoid these pitfalls:

Mistake 1: Waiting Until the Last Minute

Problem: Many students only seek help when they’re failing—leading to cramming and poor results.

Solution:

  • Use tutoring proactively, not reactively.
  • Schedule sessions before tests (not the night before).
  • Review past mistakes to prevent future failures.

Example: *A student who waited until final exams to use tutoring only got partial help—whereas a student who started early aced the course.

Mistake 2: Not Being Specific with Questions

Problem: Vague questions like "I don’t get this problem" waste both your and the tutor’s time.

Solution:

  • Break problems into smaller parts (e.g., "Can you explain step 1?").
  • Show your work so the tutor can see where you’re stuck.
  • Use the "5 Whys" technique to dig deeper.

Example: Instead of: "I don’t understand this chemistry problem." Try: "I don’t get why we balance the equation this way. Can you walk me through the steps?"

Problem: Some students think tutors will do all the work for them.
