Chapter 1: Getting Started with Montclair State University Libraries

Before diving into research or study sessions, it’s essential to familiarize yourself with the library’s structure, services, and tools. Here’s how to set yourself up for success from day one.

1.1 Understanding the Library’s Physical & Digital Layout

Montclair State University Libraries span multiple campuses, including:

  • Library & Learning Commons (LLC) – The main hub on the Upper Montclair campus, featuring study rooms, computer labs, and a café.
  • Herman A. Schwartz Library – A specialized branch with law, business, and education resources.
  • Special Collections & University Archives – Home to rare books, historical documents, and university records.

Pro Tip: Use the Montclair State University Libraries Map to plan your visits. The LLC is the most student-friendly, with 24/7 access (via keycard) and tech support desks for troubleshooting.

1.2 Your First Visit: What to Expect

Your first trip to the library should be strategic. Here’s what to do:

✅ Locate the Circulation Desk – This is where you’ll check out books, renew loans, and get your student ID card (required for borrowing). ✅ Find the Research Help Desk – Librarians here can guide you on finding sources, using databases, and citing properly. ✅ Explore the Library Catalog – Before visiting, search the Montclair State University Libraries Catalog to see what’s available. ✅ Check Out Tech Lending – Need a laptop, camera, or headphones? The library offers free equipment checkout for students.

Real-World Example: A first-year biology student, Sarah, struggled to find peer-reviewed articles for her research paper. On her first visit, she stopped by the Research Help Desk, where a librarian taught her how to use JSTOR and ScienceDirect—saving her hours of frustration.

1.3 Setting Up Your Library Account

To access e-books, databases, and borrowing privileges, you must activate your library account:

  1. Log in via Montclair State University Libraries Portal using your Montclair State username and password.
  2. Verify your student status (faculty/staff will need their university credentials).
  3. Check your loan history and renew books online to avoid late fees.

Common Mistake: Many students forget to log in with their university credentials, leading to access errors. Always use montclair.edu email and password.

2.2 How to Find E-Books Efficiently

Searching for e-books can be time-consuming if you don’t know the right tricks. Here’s how to locate them fast:

Real-World Example: A graduate student in education, James, needed a specific textbook for his seminar. Since Montclair didn’t have it, he used ILL to borrow it from another university—receiving it within 48 hours without extra cost.

✅ Use the Library’s "Find It" Button – When searching in Google Scholar or a database, look for a "Find It at Montclair" link. This will take you to the full-text version if available. ✅ Check for Open Access (OA) Versions – Some journals offer free PDFs on platforms like ResearchGate or Academia.edu. ✅ Use the Library’s Journal Finder – Enter the journal title in Montclair’s Journal Finder to see if Montclair subscribes.

Common Mistake: Students often try to download PDFs from random websites, which can lead to malware or copyright violations. Always use official library databases.

Chapter 3: Research Strategies for Success

Research can be daunting, especially when you’re new to academic writing. Montclair’s libraries offer expert guidance to help you find credible sources, avoid plagiarism, and structure your work effectively.

3.1 How to Develop a Research Question (With Examples)

A strong research question is the foundation of any successful paper. Here’s how to craft one:

  1. Start Broad – "What are the effects of social media on mental health?"
  2. Narrow It Down – "How does Instagram use among teens correlate with anxiety disorders?"
  3. Make It Testable – "Can cognitive behavioral therapy reduce Instagram-related anxiety in high school students?"

Actionable Tip: Use the CRAAP Test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) to evaluate sources. Ask:

  • Is this source recent enough for my topic?
  • Does it directly relate to my research?
  • Who wrote it? Are they credible experts?
  • Are there facts, data, or citations to support claims?
  • What’s the purpose? Is it informative, persuasive, or biased?

3.2 Finding Primary vs. Secondary Sources

Understanding the difference between primary and secondary sources is crucial for stronger research:

Primary Source Secondary Source
Original research (e.g., a journal article reporting new data, a historical document, a survey result). Analysis or interpretation of primary sources (e.g., a textbook summary, a review article, a news article).
Example: A 1920s newspaper clipping about the Great Depression. Example: A modern history textbook explaining the Great Depression.

Real-World Example: *A history student, Priya, needed primary sources for her thesis on Women’s Suffrage. She found original letters from suffragettes in Montclair’s Special Collections, which gave her unique insights that secondary sources couldn’t provide.

3.3 Avoiding Plagiarism: Citing Like a Pro

Plagiarism is one of the biggest academic risks, but proper citation can be overwhelming. Here’s how to do it correctly:

✅ Use Montclair’s Citation Guides – The library offers APA, MLA, and Chicago style guides at Montclair’s Citation Help Page. ✅ Try Zotero or Mendeley – These free citation managers help you organize sources and auto-generate citations. ✅ Paraphrase Smartly – Don’t just change a few words; rewrite in your own words while keeping the original meaning. ✅ Use Quotation Marks for Direct Quotes – Always cite the exact source if you use someone else’s words verbatim.

Common Mistake: Students often forget to cite paraphrased ideas, thinking only direct quotes need references. Always cite any borrowed information.

4.3 Avoiding Distractions in the Library

Even in a quiet study zone, distractions can kill productivity. Here’s how to stay focused:

✅ Use Noise-Canceling Headphones – Block out background chatter with white noise or instrumental music. ✅ Set a Timer (Pomodoro Technique) – Work for 25 minutes, then take a 5-minute break. ✅ Avoid Multitasking – Close unnecessary tabs and stick to one task at a time. ✅ Use the Library’s "Focus Zones" – These areas are explicitly for quiet, deep work.

Common Mistake: Students often sit in noisy areas thinking they’ll "get used to it." If you’re easily distracted, choose a silent zone instead.
